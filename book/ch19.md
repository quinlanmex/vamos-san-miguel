# Chapter 19: Dining & Fitness

## The workout you never scheduled

The morning it clicked, I was winding through San Miguel de Allende's cobblestones just after sunrise. The air was cool enough to wake you up and warm enough that you left the jacket at home. Shopkeepers were sweeping their doorsteps, a rooster was making its case from some hidden courtyard, and the smell of fresh tortillas drifted out of a corner stand. By the time I sat down for breakfast at Inside Cafe, I had covered more ground than I used to on a full day in Jacksonville, and I had not "gone for a workout" once.

That is the promise most people arrive expecting to fight for. You pack your guilt next to your running shoes and brace for months of either rigid discipline or total surrender, the same war between staying healthy and eating well that you fought back home. Mexico can dissolve that war. It does not do it for you, though, and it does not do it everywhere.

Here is the honest version, and the reason this chapter exists. The lifestyle that quietly rebuilt my daily rhythm is available to you, but it is a choice, not a climate. Pick a sprawling suburb outside Mexico City or Guadalajara and you inherit the same car-dependent day that wears people down north of the border. Pick somewhere walkable, ideally somewhere built before cars decided how streets should work, and the movement folds itself into ordinary life until you stop calling it exercise. The rest of this chapter is about making both halves of that life, the moving and the eating, deliberate instead of accidental.

## The walking you don't notice

Start with the objection, because it is a fair one. Walking is not a fitness plan. You cannot stroll your way to the body composition you want, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

True. And also beside the point for what walking actually does here, which is change the baseline you live at every single day.

Back in suburban Jacksonville, my wife and I forced ourselves to the gym three times a week and still felt sedentary the other four. Six weeks into Mexico City we were covering roughly three times the daily distance without once framing it as exercise. Willpower had nothing to do with it. American suburbs punish curiosity, since there is rarely anywhere interesting to walk to and no safe way to get there on foot. Mexican neighborhoods reward it. Every morning walk turned into a small treasure hunt. Which vendor had the ripest mangoes, whether the coffee shop with the good cortado had finally opened, what had changed on the wall art overnight. Those questions pulled me out the door more reliably than any fitness app ever managed.

The terrain does half the work. Mexico's 177 pueblos mágicos, towns the federal government recognizes for their beauty, history, or legend, tend to be exactly the kind of place that makes an efficient route impossible. [1] Guanajuato's maze of colonial streets and old silver-mining tunnels can turn a walk to the pharmacy into a climb to a viewpoint over the whole city. In Sayulita a beach walk means picking your way over tide pools and volcanic rock while surfers thread past with their boards. Choose a town built on hills, which describes San Miguel de Allende, Puerto Vallarta, Valle de Bravo, and a long list of others, and a grocery run becomes something close to interval training whether you signed up for it or not.

There is a quieter benefit that has nothing to do with step counts. When you walk out of curiosity rather than obligation, the nervous system settles in a way that supports everything else. Cortisol eases off, breathing slows down, and the stress patterns that make weight so hard to manage in the first place start to loosen.

Now, the claim you will hear repeated at expat happy hours, that Mexicans are simply thinner because they move more. Handle it with care. Mexican adult obesity runs a few points below the U.S. figure, 36.1% against 40.3%, and both countries sit near the top of the world tables, not the bottom. [2][3] The gap is real but modest, and plenty of factors sit behind it. What you can verify with your own two feet is narrower and more useful: in a walkable town, movement stops being a decision you have to win every morning and becomes the shape of the day. That is the part you get to keep.

## Getting stronger, from local gyms to a living-room kettlebell

Walking will not maintain the muscle and metabolic flexibility that matter more with every passing decade. For that you have to lift something heavy on purpose, and Mexico makes that cheaper and less annoying than it was back home.

Every neighborhood seems to have options, from bare-bones powerlifting caves charging 200 pesos a month to polished chains like Sports World that would not look out of place in Miami. The sweet spot usually sits in between, a locally owned gym with solid equipment and an owner who learns your name by the second week. Neighborhood gyms typically run 300 to 800 pesos a month, roughly $15 to $40, for access that would cost $150 or more in a major U.S. city. Mexico's national average for a monthly membership lands around 680 pesos, so those numbers hold up outside my own block. [4] Personal training costs a fraction of U.S. rates too, often 200 to 400 pesos a session, about $10 to $20, against $75 to $150 back home.

The culture is the part that surprised me. Friends describe rooms with less mirror posing and more actual movement, where a construction worker, a doctor, and a retiree train side by side without the quiet status games common in American fitness chains. The low price helps in a way that sounds backwards. When a membership is cheap, you feel no pressure to extract your money's worth from every visit, and that lack of pressure makes you more likely to just show up. If you land somewhere with a country club, the same logic applies to its gym and courts. Use whatever facility you will actually walk into on a Tuesday.

I did not use any of it for the first six weeks, which brings me to my Bowflex kettlebell. It sat in my Mexico City living room as the most expensive doorstop in the country while I promised myself I would start "tomorrow," once I found the perfect routine. The perfect routine never showed up. What broke the standoff was typing "20 minute kettlebell routine" into YouTube out of pure frustration. Twenty minutes. Even world-class procrastination cannot build a case against twenty minutes.

The adjustable design is what makes it work in a Mexican apartment. Mine ranges from 8 to 40 pounds, small enough to tuck into a corner and heavy enough for years of progressive overload, and paired with follow-along videos it erased every excuse at once. No commute, no schedule conflict, no equipment shortage, no monthly fee. A yoga mat turned out to be just as essential, and not mostly for yoga. Mexican apartments love a gorgeous tile or concrete floor, and gorgeous tile punishes anything you try to do lying down. The mat marks out a workout space and spares your joints, which is enough to turn any room into a passable studio.

## The magnificent maze of Mexican dining

Three weeks in, I made the classic gringo mistake and paid the classic tuition. The taco stand looked perfect. Long line of locals, meat sizzling, tortillas warming on the comal. I ordered with confidence, ate with enthusiasm, and spent the next 48 hours learning exactly why crowd-following is not a food safety system.

The lesson was worth the two bad days. I learned to read a stall on purpose rather than by vibe. High turnover means the ingredients have not been sitting out. Visible preparation lets you judge the workspace before you commit. A genuinely busy vendor cannot afford to make regulars sick and stay busy. I also made peace with the fact that an occasional stomach adjustment is part of the immersion and not a reason to retreat to the sanitized safety of a chain.

This matters more now that the food itself is getting its due. In 2024, Michelin published its first guide to Mexico, covering Mexico City, Oaxaca, Baja California, Baja California Sur, Quintana Roo, and Nuevo León, and it has since expanded to states including Jalisco, Puebla, and Yucatán. [5] The guide validated what anyone paying attention already knew. Nixtamalization, the long refinement of mole, the craft behind good mezcal, these represent centuries of technique that stand next to any culinary tradition on earth. Recognition ripples outward. High-end kitchens gain international credibility, mid-level places raise their game, and even street vendors feel the pull upward. The best food, though, was often exceptional long before any inspector arrived: the mole negro specialist in Oaxaca working from her grandmother's method, the al pastor stand that draws a line around the block every Tuesday.

Part of why that diversity exists is a system that would give an American health inspector chest pains. In much of Mexico, anyone with a recipe, a folding table, and enough nerve can open a food business. Little to no permitting, no routine inspection, just direct accountability to customers who vote with their wallets and their stomachs. The upside is extraordinary variety and invention. The elote cart outside a metro station might run thirteen spice blends and five sauces. The tamale seller who appears on your corner each Saturday might carry twenty regional varieties. The tradeoff needs saying plainly, because there is no health department standing behind any of it. Food safety becomes your job, which is why learning to read a vendor is a skill worth building deliberately in your first few months rather than after your first bad night.

## Paying, and the tip that made a waiter chase me

Payment caught up faster than anyone predicted. Vendors who ran cash-only for decades now wave a phone to take a transfer. Mid-level restaurants process cards without blinking. High-end places accept anything you can produce. That flexibility quietly removed the old friction, the hunt for an ATM before you could try a promising stand, the awkward math of splitting a check in exact change. Cash still earns its keep, though. Some vendors shave a little off the price for it, and a cash tip is always welcome even when a card handles the main bill.

Which brings me to the evening a waiter chased me three blocks down Insurgentes, waving a fistful of bills and looking worried. Two months in, feeling fluent in restaurant etiquette, I had left a 25% tip on an 800-peso dinner by pure U.S. reflex. To him it looked like I had forgotten my change on the table.

Mexican tipping runs on different math, and it is not hard once you know the shape of it.

| Tip | What it signals |
| --- | --- |
| 10% | The baseline. Adequate for standard, attentive service without being generous. Treated as close to mandatory. |
| 15% | The sweet spot, and my default. Appreciative without reading as tone-deaf, and it reliably earns a warm response. |
| 20% | The edge of excessive. Reserve it for service that genuinely went out of its way. |

Paying by card, you can state the tip up front to skip the awkward calculation. "Más quince por ciento, por favor" tells the server to add 15% and signals that you know how this works. Cash tips are appreciated but not required, whatever a guidebook may have told you.

## The choreography of a Mexican meal

A Mexican restaurant runs on an unspoken rhythm, and knowing it turns you from an obvious tourist into a comfortable regular. The whole sequence, from door to street, is predictable enough to learn in an evening.

| Moment | What to say | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Menu | "Una carta, por favor" | Most servers offer it without prompting. |
| Need help reading it | "¿Puede ayudarme con el menú?" | Most servers enjoy this when they sense curiosity rather than entitlement. |
| Drinks first | They ask "¿Algo para tomar?" | Note "tomar," not "beber." Drinks come before food, always. |
| Water | "agua natural" or "agua mineral" | Still or sparkling. Specify, or you get whatever they assume. |
| Ready to order | "Ya estamos listos" | No one will rush you toward it. Use the time to ask questions. |
| Dessert | "postres" | Worth a look even if you pass. Tres leches and cacao-forward sweets do things you will not find elsewhere. |
| The check | "La cuenta, por favor" | It will not arrive on its own. Bringing it early reads as pushing you out. |
| Paying by card | "Un terminal, por favor" | The server brings the machine to your table. |
| Cash, need change | "Necesito cambio, por favor" | Leave cash in the folder only if you do not need any back. |
| Receipt | "¿Su ticket?" or "¿Su comprobante?" | Keep it for business meals, skip it for casual ones. |
| Restroom | "¿Dónde está el baño?" | Quality ranges from pristine to adventurous. Ask anyway. |

None of this is a test. Servers here do not treat the meal as a transaction to complete quickly, and once you stop expecting American efficiency, the unhurried pace becomes the best part of eating out.

## Knowing which door to walk through

Mexico's dining runs a full spectrum, and matching the tier to the occasion saves you both money and misfires.

| Tier | What you get | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Street vendors | Immediate preparation, ultra-fresh ingredients, generations-old recipes, prices in pesos | The most authentic food and best value, once you have the confidence and the stomach for it |
| Mom-and-pop | Family recipes, neighborhood regulars, servers who learn your order, everyday prices | The foundation of your local dining life |
| Local higher-end | Chef-driven menus, real technique, wine that complements the food | Date nights, hosting visitors, celebrations |
| Mexican chains (Sanborns, VIPs, El Portón) | Predictable quality with local character | Business meetings, or easing a nervous first-time visitor in |
| International chains (McDonald's, Starbucks, Domino's) | Exactly what they are everywhere | Homesick moments. Use sparingly, since they skip the whole point of being here |

## Erasing the false choice

It is easy to arrive carrying an invisible assumption: that staying healthy means fighting delicious food every day. That assumption is the problem itself, because the stress it creates makes both the eating and the moving worse. Mexico can retire that fight, but only if you set the life up on purpose rather than hoping the country does it for you.

The threats are real and specific. Expat weight creeps in fast when you lean into the dining and let the movement slide. Uber dependency in Mexico City can quietly halve your daily steps. A week at a resort can undo months of habit. The answer is to build systems that make the good choice the default, so you are not relitigating it every morning. Walking becomes your first-choice transportation whenever it is safe and practical. Strength work happens somewhere you will actually return to, whether that is a gym, a club, or a mat on your living room floor. Dining exploration follows guidelines you have tested rather than pure impulse.

The incentives line up in your favor for once. A gym membership costs less than monthly parking did back home. Fresh market produce costs a fraction of the packaged version. Walking saves transportation money while it works your legs. And the payoff runs past the scale, into better sleep, lower inflammation, and the kind of steady mood that comes from a day with movement built into it.

Here is the whole plan, small enough to keep.

1. **Walk daily.** Pick a route interesting enough to pull you out the door, and vary it when it goes stale. Aim for 8,000 or more steps without once calling it a workout, and make walking your default for short trips.
2. **Lift twice a week.** Gym, club, or a kettlebell in the corner, whichever one you will actually use rather than the one that sounds impressive. Twenty focused minutes, protected like an appointment. Consistency beats intensity, especially while you are still adjusting to everything else.
3. **Explore weekly.** Try one new vendor, restaurant, or dish, starting with lower-risk options and pushing the adventure up as your palate and your stomach adapt. Keep a running note of what worked, and you will build your own vendor-reading instinct within a few months.

These three reinforce each other. Walking uncovers the places worth exploring and burns the calories that make exploring guilt-free. Lifting keeps the metabolic flexibility that lets you enjoy the food. And the food gives your walks a destination worth reaching. Six months from now you will look back at the old pattern, the gym as penance and every meal as a negotiation, with the same mild bewilderment I felt on those sunrise streets in San Miguel, wondering how you ever ran your body the other way.

### Your next action

You do not need to overhaul your life this week. You need to lay one rail down so the rest has something to follow.

1. **Map one walking route from your front door** and walk it three mornings this week. If it bores you by Friday, change it. The route that keeps you curious is the one that keeps you moving.
2. **Lock in your strength system before you talk yourself out of it.** Visit two neighborhood gyms and ask their monthly rate, or buy an adjustable kettlebell and queue up one 20-minute video. Pick the option you will use on a bad day, not a good one.
3. **Learn the meal choreography and try one new place.** Memorize "Una carta, por favor," "La cuenta, por favor," and "Más quince por ciento, por favor," then go somewhere you have never eaten and use all three.
