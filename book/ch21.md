# Chapter 21: Transportation & Storage

## The Tsuru in Oaxaca

The taxi driver pulled into oncoming traffic without slowing down. His Tsuru, a car older than some of my clothes, slid into the gap between a city bus and a motorcycle carrying what looked like an entire family, and my hand found the door handle on its own. He caught my face in the mirror. "¿Todo bien?" Everything okay?

He was not being reckless. The bus had already drifted right to make room before he committed. The motorcycle had eased off the throttle. Thirty drivers were running a set of silent negotiations at 40 miles an hour, and the only person in that car who did not understand the rules was the American in the back seat clutching the door.

A few months later I was driving home from downtown Valle de Bravo, around a mountain bend, when a pair of headlights swung into my lane to finish a pass. I slowed and let the car complete it. Nobody honked. Business as usual. Somewhere in those months I had stopped reading the road as chaos and started reading it as language.

That is the useful thing to understand before you rent, buy, or board anything. Getting around Mexico works. It just does not work the way the system you grew up with works, and the sooner you learn its grammar the sooner it stops frightening you. This chapter covers the whole question: how to move (drive, bus, ride-share), whether to own a car at all, the paperwork that applies to you versus the paperwork expat forums invent, and where to put your belongings and your vehicle when you leave for a stretch.

## Reading the road

Predictability here comes from human communication, not from painted lines. Once you learn the vocabulary, a lot of what looked dangerous turns out to be routine.

- **Flashing headlights** carry the traffic. A quick flash from a driver waving you through means "go ahead." A flash back means "thank you." A flash from someone coming at you means "heads up, something ahead."
- **Hazard lights** mean danger of any kind: a stalled truck, construction, or traffic slowing hard in front of you. Drivers tap them on the instant they brake unexpectedly, and you should too.
- **Passing happens everywhere**, including double yellow lines and blind curves. The bus overtaking you on a bend knows the route better than you do and can see sight lines you cannot. Your job is to ease toward the shoulder and let it finish.
- **Your phone stays down.** A dog, a jaywalker, a vendor, or a topes (speed bump) with no warning sign can appear at any second. The whole system depends on everyone paying attention, so pay attention.

None of this is written on a sign. You absorb it by watching, and within a couple of weeks your peripheral vision does most of the work for you.

## Do you even need a car?

The first assumption most people bring down is that they need a car on day one. In a lot of places you do not, and buying one too fast is the most common transportation mistake expats make. Where you land changes the answer completely.

**Big cities: probably not.** In Mexico City my car would have sat parked most days while I took Ubers and the metro. Surface traffic through Condesa crawls at walking pace, and friends who bought expensive cars in CDMX (Ciudad de México, what locals often call the capital) mostly came to envy the people who had mastered the buses and the walking. Parking and security eat the rest of the appeal.

**Small towns and mountain towns: probably yes.** Drive two hours to Valle de Bravo and the math flips. Groceries are scattered, restaurants hide down unmarked callejones or sit in the next town over, and a medical appointment might be in Toluca. Without a car you are stuck paying for taxis or waiting on ride-share apps that may never show.

**Beach and village life: maybe neither.** In places like San Pancho or Tulum, a golf cart, a scooter, an ATV, or a bicycle with a basket often beats a car outright. Plenty of expats in smaller towns never buy anything with four wheels and a roof.

The honest move is to arrive with no car, spend your first month on Uber, buses, and your own two feet, and let your actual routine tell you whether ownership earns its keep.

## Buying local versus importing

If you decide you want a car, the next fork is whether to buy one in Mexico or bring one down.

Buying in Mexico is expensive. In a Kavak megastore outside Toluca (Kavak is Mexico's answer to Carvana, an online-first used-car dealer with fixed prices and guarantees), the Toyota I had driven in Florida for $28,000 wore a sticker of 950,000 pesos, roughly $47,000 at the time. Import duties and value-added tax do that. Mexican dealerships give you warranties, financing, and local support, and you pay a real premium for all of it.

Importing your own car sounds like the savings play, and sometimes the paperwork eats the savings. If you are a tourist on an FMM permit or a temporary resident, you can bring a foreign-plated car in on a Temporary Import Permit (TIP), issued by Banjercito or a handful of Mexican consulates. Two facts decide most of these cases. First, permanent residents cannot get a TIP at all, and if you upgrade from temporary to permanent status while your foreign-plated car is in the country, you are expected to export it. [1] Second, the TIP requires proof of a Mexican insurance policy before it will issue. [1] Unless you are bringing down something specific and hard to replace, the hassle usually outruns the benefit.

The wild card is the used market, which runs on relationships more than on platforms. A mechanic's word carries more weight than a dealer's warranty, and the best deals move through friend networks before they ever reach a listing. I have watched an expat pick up a 2019 Tahoe for $25,000 because the owner was moving back to Dallas, and another land a well-kept Prius because a Mexican family upgrading their car preferred to sell to someone they trusted.

## Find your mechanic before you buy your car

Your mecánico matters more than your dealership. A good one keeps a fifteen-year-old vehicle running for years at roughly a third of what dealer service costs, which quietly rewrites the whole economics of ownership.

Ask expat friends for referrals, then go look. Watch how a shop treats its customers, how the workspace is organized, and how they answer your questions. The mechanics worth keeping explain the problem in plain terms, show you the worn part, and hand you options instead of steering you to the priciest fix.

## The intercity bus network

Mexico's long-distance buses are one of the country's quiet triumphs. Primera Plus, ETN, and ADO run like airlines on wheels: reserved seats, reclining chairs with more legroom than domestic business class, climate control, onboard movies, working WiFi, and clean bathrooms. The Mexico City to San Miguel de Allende run costs around $40 for a comfortable four-hour ride, and the stations are modern and orderly enough to embarrass most American bus terminals.

The network reaches almost everywhere. You can travel from Tijuana to Chetumal or from Puerto Vallarta to Veracruz, usually with a single transfer, at a frequency that makes owning a car for occasional long trips look like a luxury you do not need.

## Getting around town

**Metro and light rail.** Mexico City's metro moves millions of riders a day and charges 5 pesos a ride, about a quarter, which makes it one of the cheapest subways on earth. [4] The tradeoffs are peak-hour crowding, the women-and-children cars during rush periods, and the maze of transfers at stations like Pino Suárez. Guadalajara's light rail links its main districts, and Monterrey's metro is a well-run system.

**Colectivos and route taxis.** Smaller cities run on buses, colectivos, and route taxis: shared rides that follow a fixed destination for a fixed few pesos. They take a day or two to decode and then become second nature.

## Uber, InDrive, and the taxi you already trust

Uber dominates the major cities and runs 30 to 40 percent below what you would pay in the States. InDrive, a newer app where you propose a fare and drivers accept or counter, often beats it on longer trips. Both work well in cities and thin out fast the moment you leave them.

Do not write off traditional taxis, especially radio-dispatched sitio services. They offer fixed pricing, drivers you can get to know, and local knowledge no app can match. In smaller towns the informal taxi network runs on reputation, and your hotel or landlord's recommendation is worth more than any star rating.

## The paperwork that applies to you

I once stood in the San Miguel transit office clutching my U.S. license, watching the woman ahead of me get sent from desk to desk for an hour, my palms sweating on her behalf. My friend Carlos pulled me aside and asked the only question that mattered: "¿Para qué?" Why are you doing this? I had assumed I needed to rebuild my entire American life in Spanish. Most of it, it turned out, I did not need at all.

### Your U.S. license does the job

For visiting and tourist driving, Mexico recognizes a valid U.S. driver's license outright. No translation, no international permit, no special stamp. [3] The document from Minnesota or Texas or California works exactly as issued.

The forum anxiety that pushes newcomers toward an immediate Mexican license is mostly wasted energy. Some expats get one anyway for reasons that hold up: a Mexican license works as everyday ID for banking and phone contracts without inviting questions, police stops tend to go smoother with a familiar local document, and a few insurers shave the premium for drivers who carry one. Useful, occasionally. Legally required for driving, no. (One caveat worth checking for your own state, in the note at the end of the chapter.)

### Registration, if you buy a Mexican-plated car

Buying local triggers state-level registration in the state where you will keep the car, and that bureaucracy varies a lot across Mexico. I watched a neighbor grind through Guanajuato's process across multiple offices and multiplying fees. Another friend used Pat's Plates, a Puerto Vallarta-based service that handles vehicle registration for foreigners throughout Mexico (not only in Vallarta). She handed over documents and payment on a Tuesday and had plates and registration three weeks later without setting foot in a government office. These services know which documents each state wants and which offices move quickly, and the fee, usually well under $1,000 for everything, tends to pay for itself in saved time.

### Insurance is not optional

At a Pemex station in Toluca I watched police work a fender-bender between a delivery truck and a tourist's rental. The truck driver produced a thick folder. The tourist held up his U.S. insurance card, and the officers shook their heads.

Mexican law requires Mexican coverage, and the Mexican government does not recognize U.S. or Canadian auto policies as valid proof of liability. [2] Your excellent stateside policy provides no protection here. Driving uninsured can mean impoundment, heavy fines, and detention after an accident until it is clear you can cover the costs, regardless of who was at fault. [2]

The fix is a local broker who represents several companies and can walk you through coverage in English. The fastest way to find a good one is to ask your landlord who insures their property and car, because most landlords already have an agent who understands expat situations. Mexican premiums typically run 30 to 50 percent below comparable U.S. coverage, with a small bump for foreign-plated vehicles or guaranteed English-speaking claims adjusters. Pay it.

## Storage in a country that does not do storage

Mexico will challenge your American assumptions about where things go when you are not using them. Self-storage facilities exist mostly in big cities and larger expat hubs, and even there they cost a lot relative to local wages. Most Mexicans solve storage through family, spare rooms, and shared space, and the best default for an expat is simple: keep everything at your rental home.

### The gap between leases

The hard case is the liminal month between rentals, or a stretch back in the States. Hotels are expensive for holding boxes, storage facilities may not exist where you need them, and you hesitate to dump your life in a friend's guest room.

The transitions that go smoothly are the ones planned in advance: negotiate early access to the new rental, arrange car storage with someone you trust, or rent an Airbnb chosen for its garage rather than its bedroom. When we moved from Valle de Bravo to San Miguel and spent a month in the States in between, we loaded everything we wanted to keep into our 2012 Mitsubishi Montero, parked it in a friend's garage in Mexico City, disconnected the battery, and scattered moisture absorbers through the interior. A month later we picked up our life without missing a beat.

### Storing a car for a while

Leaving the country for an extended period takes a little prep. Disconnect the battery so it does not drain. Fill the gas tank to cut down on condensation. Put moisture absorbers throughout the interior. Some people pull valuables and add security devices on top of that.

Climate decides how aggressive you need to be. Coastal humidity is far harder on a parked car than highland air, so a vehicle keeps better in the dry stretches of Baja California Sur than in the tropical damp of the Yucatán.

## The economics of movement

### The true cost of a car

Ownership carries the obvious line items (purchase, insurance, fuel, maintenance) plus the ones that hide: parking, security, and depreciation in a market with thin resale demand. Run the comparison honestly and many expats find that steady Uber use plus the occasional rental car costs less than owning while removing the headaches entirely. Four $50 Uber runs to the airport a year, plus $40-a-day rentals for road trips, often comes in under what a car costs you every month just to sit there.

## Your next action

You do not need to solve transportation before you arrive. You need to stop pre-committing to it. Three moves:

1. **Spend your first month car-free.** Use Uber, InDrive, buses, and walking, and write down what things cost and how often the lack of a car really bites. Let the data, not the anxiety, decide whether you buy.
2. **Build the relationships before you need them.** Ask expat friends for a mechanic, a trusted taxi driver, and a local insurance broker (your landlord's agent is the shortcut). Find your mechanic before you find your car.
3. **Plan every transition in advance.** Before you leave for a stretch, line up where the car and the boxes go: a friend's garage, early access to the next rental, battery disconnected, moisture absorbers in. The logistics are easy when they are arranged early and miserable when they are not.
