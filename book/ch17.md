# Chapter 17: Domestic Help

## The 8 a.m. thwack

The sound hit me at 8 a.m. sharp. A rhythmic thwack, thwack, thwack from the laundry room. I came down the stairs expecting a washing machine mid-breakdown and found Maria bent over a concrete basin, working a wooden paddle against my son's grass-stained soccer uniform. The washboard was not a museum piece. It was stain-removal technology that quietly outperforms the machine sitting three feet away.

I took my coffee back to the kitchen and listened. Through the window I could see Jorge trimming hedges with the sort of care that used to eat an entire Saturday of mine back in the States. It was a Wednesday. I had nowhere to be except at the breakfast table with my family, walking into my workday with the whole house already in order.

That morning is the entire argument of this chapter, so let me make it explicit before we get to rates and referrals. In Mexico, the cost of handing off the work you dread drops far enough that you can actually do it, and the hours you buy back are the point of moving here in the first place.

## The psychology of help

Name the objection first, because you are probably carrying it. The American script treats domestic help two ways. Either it is what you resort to when you have failed to keep up, a small admission of defeat, or it is a way to show off money you want other people to notice. Both readings make hiring someone feel slightly shameful.

Mexico starts from a different place. For most of human history, running a household was shared labor. Extended family carried part of it. Neighbors carried part of it. The idea that one person, or one couple, should privately master a career, a spotless home, well-raised kids, and the family finances all at once is recent, and it does not work well for anyone. We dressed it up as self-sufficiency. It functions more like a design flaw.

Move here and you get to opt out of that story. The math is what makes opting out possible. For the price of a single deep-clean service in the United States, you can have Maria every Friday for a month. The weekend you would have spent pressure-washing a driveway or scrubbing tile comes back to you, and those hours compound into hiking trips, work you care about, and time with the people you moved here to be near.

That is the human side of the arbitrage this book keeps returning to. Lower cost of living is part of it. The larger part is no longer doing everything alone.

## Building your support network

Start with your landlord. They are the single most valuable resource you have on day one. They have used trusted providers for years and they understand who fits with whom in a way no online review can. When we moved in, our landlord handed us three housekeeping options, two childcare providers, and a maintenance man who had cared for the property for over a decade. That one conversation erased weeks of trial and error.

More options surface as you settle in. Neighbors, parents from school and sports, and local shopkeepers all know who is reliable. Our housekeeper Maria introduced us to the gardener who fixed our backyard, and the gardener recommended the specialist who solved our water-pump problem. A web of referrals like this holds together far better than hiring cold, one stranger at a time.

Work these five moves in order:

1. **Call your landlord the day you arrive.** Ask which providers already come with the house, who else they recommend, and what the current rates are.
2. **Ask neighbors what has worked for them.** Referrals from the family next door carry weight because they know your street and your situation.
3. **Tap expat families through local groups and events.** You will find specialists other people have already vetted.
4. **Visit hardware stores and markets.** The staff know the skilled maintenance workers because those workers shop there.
5. **Talk to school communities for childcare.** Parents are glad to name the people they trust with their own kids.

## Housekeeping

Maria showed up with an arsenal that would impress a lab technician. Commercial-grade Fabuloso, Pinol, and Clorox, all of it in service of a view that treats cleanliness as a matter of dignity. The first time I watched her go after our bathroom with industrial bleach, the surfaces came out gleaming.

Then came the washboard. That ridged concrete basin pulls out stains a machine gives up on. Maria soaks the fabric, works in a soap paste, then drives it against the ridges in a steady rhythm. Grass, blood, and unidentifiable substances all lose to old technique paired with patience. Mexican housekeeping treats cleaning as a craft, generational knowledge run on top of powerful modern products, which is a long way from wiping a counter.

Services typically include:

- Deep cleaning with professional-grade disinfectants
- Laundry, including washboard stain removal
- Floor mopping with tile-specific cleaners
- Window cleaning, inside and out
- Organizing and light decluttering
- Cooking, with some providers

**Cost:** 60 to 100 pesos an hour, roughly $3 to $5. Full-day sessions run 500 to 800 pesos, about $25 to $40. For context, Mexico's legal minimum wage for domestic workers in 2026 is 342.47 pesos a day, close to 43 pesos an hour, set by the national wage commission CONASAMI [1]. The rates above sit comfortably above that floor, which is part of why the work is good. Rates vary by city and climb over time, but the gap with U.S. prices holds.

**Important:** Do not assume pet care is included. Talk through comfort levels with animals up front so expectations are clear.

## Childcare

Mafer had been watching neighborhood kids for fifteen years by the time she started caring for our son. Her references were not written testimonials. They were decades of family friendships. Parents stopped her on the street to thank her. Children she had raised as toddlers were bringing their own babies to meet "Tía Mafer."

I came home one afternoon to find our son teaching Mafer English words while she corrected his Spanish pronunciation, both of them laughing at the other's attempts. Mexican childcare providers tend to fold into the family and stay connected long after the kids outgrow the arrangement. They see themselves as allies rather than contractors, invested in your child's language, development, and sense of the culture. That matters more than any line on a resume.

Services typically include:

- Multi-generational childcare experience
- Spanish language and cultural education
- Flexible scheduling and emergency care
- Light household tasks during nap times
- Long-term family relationship building

**Cost:** 60 to 100 pesos an hour, roughly $3 to $5. Overnight care is negotiable.

**Vetting essentials:** Arrange a trial day before you commit to ongoing care. It protects both sides and sets clear expectations about quality, routines, communication, and emergencies.

## Maintenance

When our water pump started sounding like a freight train coming off the rails at midnight, I assumed expensive repairs and several sleepless nights. Miguel arrived the next morning with a plan. I watched him measure the concrete walls around the pump house, sketching calculations on the back of a receipt while humming to himself.

Instead of replacing the system, he built a custom soundproof enclosure out of materials that cost less than one hour of a U.S. plumber's time. The three-day job rolled electrical work, carpentry, and acoustic engineering into one, the kind of thing that would mean three separate specialists back home. Mexican maintenance workers tend to span several trades, which makes them versatile problem-solvers who engineer around a problem rather than just patching its symptom.

Services commonly include:

- Plumbing repairs and installations
- Electrical work and fixture installation
- Carpentry and furniture assembly
- Appliance repair and maintenance
- Creative solutions for unusual problems
- Preventive maintenance recommendations

**Cost:** 80 to 120 pesos an hour, roughly $4 to $6. Materials are charged separately.

**Important:** Settle who buys the supplies before work starts, so nobody is guessing about project cost or timeline.

## Drivers

Carlos showed up at 3:30 a.m. for our 6 a.m. flight with coffee and a smile that suggested he genuinely did not mind the hour. The smell of fresh coffee mixed with leather seats and cool morning air as he loaded a family of five and two weeks of luggage. I sank into the passenger seat, watched the streetlights blur past, and sipped a still-warm cup instead of white-knuckling through airport traffic.

This looks like an indulgence until you notice what it actually removes. I was not navigating unfamiliar roads, managing everyone's nerves about traffic, or arriving frazzled. That mental bandwidth stayed free. Airport runs demand early starts and close attention at exactly the hour your brain is least sharp, so handing off that one task takes a reliable weekly friction point off the board.

Some expat friends in bigger cities keep a permanent driver who uses the family car for regular errands. That works well for families who would rather not fight peak traffic or who make frequent airport runs, and the benefit grows once you count the navigation stress and decision fatigue of unfamiliar routes in high-stakes moments.

Services typically include:

- Airport transfers with luggage help
- Regular weekly transportation for groceries and appointments
- Special event transportation
- Flexible scheduling around family needs

**Cost:** 100 to 160 pesos an hour, roughly $5 to $8. Flat rates vary by distance and availability.

## Yard work

Jorge arrives every Tuesday with tools worn smooth from years of use. He looks over the plants, checks for pests, then works with a methodical calm while I get on with other things. Cut grass drifts through the open windows, punctuated by his quiet whistling.

Mexican yard work handles the fundamentals efficiently. Mowing, hedge trimming, weeding, watering, and seasonal cleanup. Most rental properties fold basic upkeep into the lease, covering weekly mowing, hedge trimming, and garden care. Anything more specialized can be negotiated.

Services include:

- Weekly lawn mowing and edge trimming
- Hedge and shrub maintenance
- Garden weeding and plant care
- Seasonal planting guidance
- Leaf and debris removal

**Cost:** Often included with rent. Additional landscaping runs 60 to 100 pesos an hour, roughly $3 to $5.

## Payment schedules and relationships

Payment works differently here. Cash is standard, and you should discuss frequency up front rather than let it become an awkward conversation later. Each type of work has its own rhythm.

| Service | Typical payment schedule |
| --- | --- |
| Housekeeping | End of each session, or weekly for regular service |
| Childcare | Weekly for ongoing care, daily for occasional sitting |
| Maintenance | On project completion |
| Yard work | Weekly or monthly for regular upkeep |
| Drivers | End of each trip, or monthly for a standing arrangement |

Now the holiday bonus, and here the friendly advice you will hear from other expats is incomplete. People will tell you a week's extra pay at the holidays is a nice gesture but optional. As a matter of law, it is not optional for domestic workers. Mexico's Federal Labor Law requires an aguinaldo of at least 15 days' wages, paid by December 20, and the requirement explicitly covers household staff [2]. Plenty of expat arrangements run informally and skip it, but you should budget for it as a real obligation rather than a tip. Paid in full and on time, it strengthens the relationship and buys you priority when the schedule gets tight around the holidays. Treat it as an investment in reliability, because that is what it is.

## Professional clarity protects the relationship

The warmth you feel with Mexican service providers is real, and it runs differently from the transactional relationships you are used to. That difference is exactly why you have to manage expectations out loud. Warmth and clarity are not in tension. Clarity is what lets the warmth last. When expectations stay vague, quiet resentment builds. When they are explicit, both sides relax because they know where they stand.

Handle these before they turn into problems:

- **Scope creep.** Define the specific tasks. "Deep cleaning" means different things to different people, so walk through a checklist on the first day and save yourself months of low-grade misalignment.
- **Schedule.** Confirm arrival time, duration, and frequency. If you need someone to stay late or work a holiday, raise it separately with extra pay.
- **Replacement coverage.** If your regular provider cannot make it, do you expect them to send someone? Most will, but confirm it so you are not caught short.
- **Saying no.** If someone asks for additional work or wants to bring a family member along, you can decline. A clear no strengthens the working relationship rather than damaging it.
- **Kind correction.** When something is off, say so. Driver goes too fast? Tell him. Cleaner keeps missing the same corner? Point it out, kindly.

Fair pay, on schedule, matters more than perfect paperwork. Cash is normal here, and paying consistently and on time is how you signal respect. That reliability becomes the foundation everything else rests on.

## The reality of variance

Everything above, the skilled work and the genuine investment in your family, is common in Mexico. It is not guaranteed. Some workers are purely transactional. They do competent work and leave. Some need closer supervision. Some vanish after a few weeks. Quality climbs sharply with careful referrals and continuity, but you will not hit it on every hire.

The difference between excellent help and mediocre help usually traces back to how you found the person. A landlord's personal referral or a friend's long-standing worker carries real weight. A random marketplace contact carries almost none. That is the whole reason the network matters. You are not looking to find good help once. You are building a referral system that compounds.

## The real return

Six months into life in Mexico, we had not done any serious yard work or deep cleaning ourselves. Those hours went back into family adventures and creative projects, which was the life that drew us here in the first place.

The washboard still gets me. Every few weeks that thwack, thwack, thwack reminds me how often modern life invents an expensive fix for a problem people solved generations ago with a simple tool and real skill. Here, asking for help reads as good sense rather than weakness.

Here is what actually happens when you reclaim those hours. Your income optionality widens. Burnout backs off. Your earning runway lengthens because you are not collapsing into exhaustion. Family stability improves, which heads off the downstream emergencies that quietly drain wealth. A rested parent makes better decisions, and a present parent runs into fewer behavioral and school problems. Fewer crises means more of your money moving toward your goals instead of toward firefighting.

That is the piece of the geographic arbitrage no spreadsheet captures. You accumulate money, yes. You also accumulate time, clarity, and margin, the space between what you are doing and what would break you. That margin is where the real wealth builds.

### Your next action

1. **Call your landlord within your first week** and ask the three questions: which providers come with the house, who else they recommend, and what the current rates are.
2. **Run a trial day before committing** to any housekeeper, childcare provider, or driver, and walk through a task checklist on that first day.
3. **Budget the aguinaldo now.** Set aside at least 15 days' pay per household worker, due by December 20, so the holiday obligation is money you already have rather than a scramble.
