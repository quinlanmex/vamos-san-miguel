# Chapter 11: Safety Rewired

## The pork quesadilla

Our trajinera drifted through the canals of Xochimilco, past the old floating gardens and the towering cypress. Vendors poled their own boats alongside ours, comals sizzling, and the smell reached us before the food did: garlic, pork, charred tortilla.

My sister-in-law's boyfriend from Denver, call him Jake, was bouncing in his seat. "This is incredible. And I'm getting those pork quesadillas."

"Jake, maybe stick with cheese," I said. "The setup on that boat."

"It's fine," he cut in, already waving the vendor over. "I can watch them cook it. The heat kills the bacteria."

The rest of us traded a look. We had lived in Mexico long enough to know what we were watching, which was American confidence walking straight into street-food reality.

Three days later Jake was folded up in his hotel room, and his last day in Mexico City turned into a study of intestinal warfare that trailed him back to Denver for the better part of three months.

That quesadilla is the whole chapter in one bite. Americans arrive worried about the wrong things. They read about cartels and then rinse a toothbrush under the tap without a second thought. They picture a dramatic threat that lives on geography they will never set foot on, and they walk right past the boring daily one that lands in their body. The point of the next several pages is to flip that around, so you spend your worry where it changes an outcome and stop spending it where it does not.

## Mexico is not one place

Start with the error underneath every other error, which is treating Mexico as a single thing with a single safety level.

Mexico covers about 1,972,550 square kilometers, the thirteenth-largest country in the world, organized into 32 states [1]. To an American eye, the scale is easy to miss. Add up the total area of Texas, New Mexico, Colorado, and Arizona and you land near 1,575,000 square kilometers, and Mexico is still larger than all four combined [2]. A country that size does not have one security profile any more than the United States does. It has dozens, set by geography, economics, and local infrastructure.

The U.S. State Department makes the point better than any expat can, because its advisory is written state by state rather than country-wide. As of the August 2025 update, Mexico as a whole sits at Level 2, "Exercise Increased Caution." Underneath that, six states carry the top-level "Level 4: Do Not Travel" designation: Colima, Guerrero, Michoacán, Sinaloa, Tamaulipas, and Zacatecas. At the other end, Campeche and Yucatán sit at Level 1, the same "Exercise Normal Precautions" rating the department gives most of the world [1]. Sinaloa reads the way it does because it is a trafficking corridor. San Miguel de Allende, in Guanajuato and the better part of a thousand kilometers away, runs on a different logic entirely, the kind where a neighborhood WhatsApp chat decides who waters whose plants while a family is traveling.

You already accept this at home. Baltimore recorded 263 homicides in 2023, a rate of about 46 per 100,000 residents, a number that would lead the evening news for a week if it belonged to a foreign city [3]. Scottsdale, Arizona, most of a country away, is a resort town where the security debate is about golf-course access and property lines. Same nation, same headlines, completely different lives, and the variable is geography. Calling Mexico "dangerous" because Sinaloa exists works exactly as well as calling America "dangerous" because Baltimore exists. In both cases the sentence erases the only thing that matters: which town, which neighborhood, which street. Your safety math is set by where you actually live, and that is as true north of the border as south of it.

## Real risk versus the risk you feel

Here is the inversion laid out plainly. The left-hand fear and the right-hand reality rarely match.

| Risk category | Perceived severity | Actual daily impact | Your control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Violent crime in trafficking corridors | Very high | Near zero (wrong geography) | High |
| Water contamination | Medium | Very high | High |
| Petty crime in expat zones | Medium | Low | High |
| Food-borne illness | Low | High | High |
| Night highway driving | Medium | Medium | High |
| Medical emergencies | High | Low | Medium |

Read down the two middle columns and the pattern is hard to unsee. The threats the media trains you to fear operate where you will not be, and the ones that will shape your first six months are almost entirely inside your control. They are just invisible to American instinct, because nobody back home ever had to think about them.

A small illustration of the upside. Our teenagers move around San Miguel de Allende with more independence than they had in our American suburb. They walk to friends' houses, go to homeschool meetups, and hang around the public library. The worry we carried in the States, the low background hum about school shootings or random violence, is simply gone. The visible security presence, including trucks with armed personnel, reads to most expats as reassurance rather than alarm.

Mexico's expat hubs run on community-based security instead of urban anonymity. Everyone knows everyone, most homes and shops have cameras, and shopkeepers remember your face and your order. This is not uniform, and Mexico City's Roma is not a small town in Guerrero, but across the main expat areas the pattern holds. One morning a police officer knocked on our door in San Miguel, wished me buenos días, apologized for the interruption, and asked whether she could see our Ring footage after a motorcycle theft that morning. Between our video and a few neighbors' cameras, a local shopkeeper later told us the thief had been picked up by that afternoon. That is the texture of crime in an expat-friendly neighborhood. Most of it is non-violent petty theft, and what does happen gets worked with real urgency and modern tools.

## The real safety framework

The foundation of your safety system is not a wall or an alarm. It is your digestive health, which will touch your daily life more than any other risk on the list. Once we had these systems dialed in, we stopped thinking about safety and started living. The rest of this chapter is the framework, built in five layers.

### Level 1: daily water and food

The water in Mexico is not sourced from anywhere strange. It comes from springs, rainfall, and aquifers, the same as anywhere. The issue is treatment and storage.

Water usually arrives treated, then gets pumped into tinacos, the big plastic tanks that sit on rooftops baking in the sun. Heat, standing water, and plastic biofilm add up to a bacterial load, and even a recently cleaned tank (most are not cleaned) drifts back toward growth because sun plus plastic plus stagnation always does. This is not carelessness on anyone's part. It is how Mexican residential plumbing solved water-pressure problems long before routine testing existed. Understanding the mechanism is what turns water management from suburban paranoia into a habit you keep without resenting it.

Daily water management:

- If your town has it, rent a place with filtration, either whole-house or an under-sink reverse-osmosis unit.
- Default to bottled water, *agua natural* (still) or *agua mineral* (sparkling).
- Keep your mouth closed in the shower.
- Brush your teeth with filtered water and keep a bottle by the sink.
- Large *garrafones* (5-gallon jugs) run about 120 pesos to start, then 50 to 60 pesos per refill. Bonafont (more mineral-rich) and Ciel (neutral) are reliable brands.

Restaurant selection:

- Lean toward higher-end places that are likely to run proper filtration.
- When unsure, ask about their water filtration before you order.
- With street vendors, choose the ones with high turnover. Fast ingredient rotation and heavy foot traffic mean the locals have already tested the place for you.

Probiotic defense. What follows is our family's personal protocol, not medical advice. Talk to your own doctor about your situation, and especially about your kids.

I once went through a three-month stretch of low-grade gut trouble and a persistent brain fog. My wife and kids were fine, so I chalked it up to middle age. Then my wife suggested I restart Enterogermina, and inside 48 hours it cleared. That is how quietly this stuff operates.

- **Enterogermina.** We buy it on arrival (4 billion UFC for adults, 2 billion for kids) and keep it stocked. After clearing it with our doctor, our approach has been one every morning before eating. When gut trouble shows up, it tends to settle things within 24 to 48 hours.
- **Saccharomyces boulardii.** NOW brand, 5 billion CFU. Again after talking with our doctor, adults take two capsules first thing in the morning (at least an hour before food), and the kids take one in the morning and one in the afternoon.

These are the exact protocols we have used ourselves. Do not copy them blind. Run them past your doctor first.

Home food safety:

- Wash all produce with Microdyn or a similar disinfectant. Add the recommended drops to a large bowl of water, soak for 15 minutes, then rinse with filtered water.
- Consider a home that already has reverse osmosis, or install it yourself. A full setup runs about $1,500, which spread over a year is roughly $125 a month on top of rent, a fair price for not thinking about it.
- Remineralize heavily filtered water with something like Panakos deep-sea drops.

### Level 2: risky foods and smart swaps

The best food in Mexico is cooked. The riskiest food is rinsed.

Vegetables washed in untreated water, green juices from an unknown kitchen, and fresh salsa left warm on a counter are the vectors. Food served hot, established restaurants whose water systems you have checked, and packaged goods from major brands are the safe list. Build your early confidence on the safe list, then widen the circle as you learn which vendors and restaurants have earned it.

One heuristic saves you a lot of mental effort in the first months: cooked food from an established, popular vendor is usually safe, cooked food from an empty stall is a gamble, and rinsed vegetables are always a gamble. That is your filter until you have built the relationships that tell you which exceptions are worth making.

### Level 3: your local safety network

Get this layer right and you avoid most of the preventable problems that derail expat transitions.

Your real safety system is the web of people who know you: vendors, restaurant owners, neighbors. Known faces get better service and, more importantly, early word about which streets or situations to skip.

Week-one moves:

- Learn your neighbors' names and numbers.
- Join the WhatsApp *colonia* chat. This, not sirens or apps, is how neighborhood safety runs day to day. Someone always knows about the pothole, the stray dog, the water outage, or the stranger who does not belong.
- Save two trusted taxi numbers as backups. Many places have solid Uber coverage and some do not, and a backup means you are never stranded at 11 p.m. working out how to get home.

Social safety comes down to the same rules it does anywhere. The high-risk behaviors travel with you: buying drugs on the street, flashing wealth in unfamiliar places, wandering alone at night in a neighborhood you do not know, drinking with strangers, waving off local advice. The low-risk daily stuff is exactly what it sounds like: walking the town center in daylight, taking registered taxis or rideshare, shopping in established markets, eating out, showing up to community events.

### Level 4: night highway driving

Do not drive the highways at night. Poor visibility, unmarked speed bumps, and unpredictable road conditions add up to a real hazard that daytime alertness will not undo. Mexico's roads vary a great deal from U.S. standards, and darkness magnifies every one of those differences. Treat it the way you would treat driving a Colorado mountain pass in a night snowstorm. Daytime driving is fine. Night driving is a risk you can simply delete from your life.

The irony writes itself. The thing most Americans never even search before they move, night driving, threatens their body more than every cartel headline they did read. That is the safety inversion in miniature.

### Level 5: emergency readiness

Learn the hospital landscape before you need it, because a crisis is a terrible time to be learning it. Mexican healthcare runs in tiers.

| Tier | Examples | What to expect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public | IMSS, ISSSTE | Solid care, long waits, heavy bureaucracy |
| Mid-tier private | Regional chains such as Ángeles and Star Médica | Balanced cost and quality, efficient, English-speaking staff at many sites |
| Top-tier private | Flagship Ángeles and high-end facilities in areas like Santa Fe in Mexico City | Highest cost, fastest service, English widely spoken |

As an expat, default to mid-tier or top-tier private care. Many international and expat-focused insurance plans are built to cover exactly these hospitals. Do not assume yours does. Call and confirm the network before you move, because your coverage details matter more than the tier itself.

Some private hospitals ask for a credit-card deposit before treatment. This is normal practice, not a warning sign, and it is how private care manages risk here. Have the conversation early. When you locate your nearest hospital, call and ask about the deposit policy. Five minutes now spares you the shock of being asked for $1,500 while you are in pain.

In a true life-threatening emergency, call 911 first (yes, Mexico uses 911 too). For everything short of that, you will usually have a better experience going straight to a vetted private hospital or doctor.

Round out the basics: pin your nearest hospital in your maps app, build a relationship with a local doctor (ask expat neighbors for names), understand how your insurance works here before you use it, and save your embassy or consulate contact in your phone.

## The enemy is accumulation, not catastrophe

One contaminated salsa will not wreck your transition. Fifteen of them will. One skipped water protocol is nothing. Three months of low-grade gut trouble is a season of your new life spent underwater.

Most expats who struggle do not crash in a single dramatic event. They accumulate fatigue, brain fog, and digestive problems, and then they blame Mexico rather than the daily protocols they never put in place. They fly home, feel ten years younger within a week, and take that as proof the country was the problem, when the real story was the missing systems.

Jake's three months could have been a cheese quesadilla. More to the point, those three months were supposed to be for building community, exploring, and settling in, and instead he spent them in a hotel room. Get the protocols right and safety stops occupying your mind at all. You just live.

## Your first weeks, in order

**This week**
- Talk to your doctor about *Saccharomyces boulardii*, and if they are comfortable, test your tolerance before the trip.
- Research water-filtration options for your target housing.
- Draft the questions you will ask hospitals about their deposit and payment policies.

**Before departure**
- Build a 7-day food-onboarding plan using known-safe restaurants only.
- Download the rideshare apps for your destination city.
- Locate the nearest hospitals in your maps app and note their deposit policies.
- Ask expat Facebook groups for trusted doctor recommendations.

**On arrival**
- Buy Enterogermina (4 billion UFC for adults, 2 billion for kids) and start the protocol you and your doctor agreed on.
- Buy Microdyn and set up your produce-cleaning routine on day one.
- Have coffee with a neighbor and ask how to join the WhatsApp *colonia* chat.
- Get two trusted taxi numbers into your phone.

**First month**
- Widen your food choices gradually while holding the water protocols steady.
- Build relationships with the vendors and restaurant staff in your neighborhood.
- Join a local group or meetup.
- Tune your probiotic routine to how you actually feel.
- Confirm your hospital and doctor relationships.

## Where safety sits in the bigger move

Roughly speaking, your Mexico safety system is about 80 percent digestive health, about 15 percent ordinary social awareness, and about 5 percent everything the internet wants you to lose sleep over. Get those proportions right and you have the foundation for everything else in this book.

Once you see that the real daily dangers, water and food and road conditions, are all manageable with systems, Mexico stops feeling risky and starts feeling calmer than the American suburb you left. The threats you can control end up buying you more confidence than the dramatic ones you cannot. Expats who get this right tend to discover they have moved somewhere that is safer in practice than it ever looked in headlines, and from there the rest of the life opens up: more freedom, lower cost, deeper community, more time with the people you moved for.

### Your next action

You do not need to master all five levels this week. You need to start the one that matters most.

1. **Book the digestive-health conversation with your doctor now**, before you fly. Ask specifically about Enterogermina and *Saccharomyces boulardii* and, if they agree, test your tolerance at home while it costs you nothing.
2. **Build the two lists that make the rest automatic:** your known-safe restaurants for week one, and your nearest hospitals with their deposit policies noted. Both are quick to assemble in advance and miserable to assemble in a crisis.
3. **Check your safety math against geography, not headlines.** Look up the State Department advisory level for the specific state you are considering [1], and weigh the daily risks you can control far more heavily than the dramatic ones you will never encounter.
