# Chapter 4: Navigating Mexican Tax Residency

> **Before we start, one thing in plain language.** This chapter explains how Mexican tax-residency law works and how the U.S.–Mexico Tax Treaty settles the fight when both countries want to claim you. It does **not** tell you what *you* owe. That answer depends entirely on your own facts, and it belongs to a CPA who does cross-border U.S.–Mexico work for a living — and, where the stakes call for it, a Mexican tax attorney (a *fiscalista*). Everything here is accurate as of early 2026 and all of it is subject to change. If you take away a single sentence, take this one: **understand the rules, work with qualified professionals, and document everything.** This book does not pick your position for you. It makes sure you know one exists.

## Mark's 2 a.m.

Mark thought he had it all figured out.

He'd done the homework most people never do. He understood the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion. He'd established Nevada domicile before he left. Federal taxes: handled. State taxes: gone. Life in Puerto Vallarta: better than the brochure. He'd optimized two of the three layers we talked about back in Chapter 1, and he felt, reasonably, like a man who had read the manual.

Then one afternoon his neighbor mentioned, the way you'd mention the weather, that she'd been paying Mexican income taxes for three years.

"Wait, what?" Mark said. "I thought we were covered under FEIE."

"FEIE covers your *U.S.* taxes," she said. "If Mexico decides you're a tax resident, you owe here too. My accountant says it's the law. Runs me about twelve grand a year."

Her accountant had reasoned that holding a residency card and living most of the year in Vallarta made her a Mexican tax resident, full stop. That's one interpretation of Mexican law. It is not the only one, and as we'll see, it skips several steps that can change the answer.

Mark's stomach dropped anyway. He spent the next week pacing the tile floors of his rental at 2 a.m., reading treaty language he didn't understand yet, wondering whether his airtight "zero-tax" life had quietly grown a five-figure hole he'd never noticed.

Here's the truth Mark didn't have that week, and the reason this chapter exists: the answer to "do I owe Mexican tax?" is genuinely unsettled for a lot of Americans living in Mexico. Not because anyone's hiding the ball — because the rules, the treaty, and the facts of how you actually live and work interact in ways that produce more than one defensible answer. The neighbor isn't obviously right. Mark isn't obviously off the hook. The point of the next several pages is to replace the 2 a.m. panic with something better: an understanding of the actual machinery, so that whatever you decide, you decide it on purpose and with your CPA, not by inheriting your neighbor's assumption over the fence.

## The practical reality: three things are true at once

Let's lay the whole board out before we move any pieces.

1. **The FEIE eliminates most or all of your U.S. federal income tax.** True for a great many expats.
2. **A no-tax-state domicile eliminates your state income tax.** Also true, and covered in Chapter 3.
3. **Whether you owe Mexican income tax is a real, open question** — and a reasonable CPA could land in one of three places:
   - Pay Mexican tax on all of your income.
   - Pay Mexican tax only on personal-services income (typically the salary you draw from an S-Corp).
   - Take the position that no Mexican tax is owed, based on non-residency and the facts of how and where your work is performed.

Which one fits *you* depends on three things: your residency status under Mexican law, your entity structure, and where your work is physically performed. It does **not** depend on your immigration status, on how many days you've spent in Mexico alone, or on what your neighbor's accountant concluded about her own situation.

You will also hear, over and over, a fourth thing from long-time expats: *"Nobody files. Mexico doesn't chase income tax from foreigners. They get plenty from us through IVA, property taxes, immigration fees, and everything our spending pumps into the local economy. Don't waste your time."*

We're not going to tell you they're wrong about enforcement, because as a description of what SAT (Mexico's tax authority) currently prioritizes, it's largely accurate. Plenty of people have operated this way for years without a knock on the door. But "currently unenforced" and "not owed" are two different statements, and quietly treating the first as if it were the second is exactly how a five-figure surprise gets built. Keep them separate in your head. This chapter helps you understand both the letter of the rules *and* the enforcement reality, so you can weigh them honestly instead of picking the comforting one.

The short version of the law, which the rest of the chapter unpacks: when your economic life is genuinely centered in the United States, Mexican domestic law generally resolves toward U.S. residency even if you spend most of the year physically in Mexico, and the treaty provides a backup layer that tends to confirm the same result. But residency is only half the analysis. Even a non-resident under both Mexican law and the treaty can have Mexican tax exposure on *personal-services income* — the salary you pay yourself through an S-Corp — depending on where that work is physically done. That second half is the part most people misunderstand, so it's the part we'll spend the most time on.

The heavy legal detail — treaty articles, the mechanics of Mexican domestic law, entity classification — lives in the Chapter 4 Appendix. The body of this chapter stays with the structure, the decision framework, and the risk management.

## One configuration people use for full-time life in Mexico

Below is one common way expats who live in Mexico full-time arrange their affairs. We're describing it, not prescribing it. Whether any of it fits you is a question for your CPA and your facts — but you can't evaluate a configuration you've never seen laid out, so here it is.

- **Days.** No more than 35 days a year in the United States, which satisfies FEIE's Physical Presence Test (330 days outside the U.S. in any rolling 12-month window). Most of the remaining year is in Mexico. Many people choose the PPT over the Bona Fide Residence test because it's purely arithmetic and sidesteps an awkward contradiction — claiming bona fide residence in Mexico while also arguing you're not a Mexican tax resident.
- **Dwelling.** A genuine U.S. dwelling in your domicile state. An actual place you can stay — not a mail-forwarding address. This is the single most load-bearing element in the whole structure. Without it, the Mexican residency analysis can resolve against you before it ever reaches the tests that make a non-residency position possible.
- **Income.** 100% U.S.-source income, flowing through U.S. accounts and U.S. contracts. No Mexican clients, no Mexican revenue, no Mexican-source income.
- **Entity structure.** An S-Corp (or an LLC taxed as one) splits your income into salary (personal services, theoretically exposed) and distributions (business profits, protected under the treaty's Article 7 permanent-establishment test). The more of your income that flows as distributions rather than salary, the smaller the slice exposed to Mexico. IRS "reasonable compensation" rules set the floor on how low the salary can go.
- **Reserve.** Each year, set aside the *theoretical* Mexican tax on your salary in a U.S. investment account, sized to the worst plausible interpretation. If the position is never challenged, or is challenged and holds, the reserve is money you kept. If it goes the other way, you're ready. (More on this below — it's the same tool whether you ultimately pay or not.)
- **Documentation.** U.S. tax returns, contracts, bank statements, a dwelling lease, voter registration, driver's license, travel records. If you run an S-Corp with U.S.-based team members, a formal Officer Delegation of Management Authority memo supporting the position that the company is effectively managed from the United States.
- **Annual U.S. visits.** One or two trips a year, 15–30 days total, at least one of them to your domicile city. Stay in your dwelling, buy groceries, visit the bank. Budget $2,000–$4,000 a year.

**One exclusion nobody should miss:** neither the FEIE nor the treaty eliminates U.S. self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare). A U.S.–Mexico Totalization Agreement was signed in 2004 but was never sent to Congress and has never entered into force. FICA applies to your worldwide self-employment income no matter where you live, and the foreign tax credit can't offset it. Budget for it. It's a real cost, and nothing in this chapter makes it go away.

## The decision you're actually making

This is not a choice between complying and not complying. Everyone in this chapter is trying to comply. It's **position selection under uncertainty** — choosing, with professional help, which defensible reading of an unsettled area fits your facts, and then backing that choice with structure and documentation.

Here's the part worth sitting with: most expats aren't actually choosing a position at all. They're *inheriting* one from the people around them — often without noticing that the inheritance came bundled with assumptions they never examined. Whatever you decide, decide it deliberately.

A position holds when three layers are intact:

- **Structure** — income source, entity type, dwelling, and day-count configuration.
- **Defensibility** — documentation, a coherent narrative, and consistency across both countries.
- **Financial containment** — a reserve sized to the worst plausible interpretation.

If all three are present, the position is economically rational whichever way it lands. If any layer is weak, the whole thing degrades fast.

### What makes a non-residency position strong or weak

If you and your CPA take a non-residency position, these are the factors that determine whether it holds up under scrutiny or falls apart. This isn't an argument for that position — it's a map of what strengthens versus weakens it if it's the one you've chosen.

| Strengthens the position | Weakens the position |
| --- | --- |
| A real U.S. dwelling | A mailbox only |
| 100% U.S.-source income | Mixed or Mexican-source income |
| Consistent documentation across both countries | Gaps or contradictions between your IRS and SAT narratives |
| A reserve sized to the worst plausible interpretation | No reserve, or one that's too small |
| Periodic U.S. visits with actual dwelling use | No U.S. visits for 3+ years |
| A delegation memo that reflects real management structure | A paper delegation that doesn't match reality |

Every item on the left strengthens a non-residency stance; every item on the right erodes it. The strength of any such position lives in the left column.

## The reserve strategy: pricing uncertainty instead of ignoring it

Uncertainty can be priced rather than avoided. It's how insurers build their models and how any business decides to proceed despite a risk it can't eliminate: estimate the exposure, set aside the cost, move forward.

Worth naming plainly: the expats who tell you "nobody files, don't bother" are running an *uninsured* version of this exact bet. They've decided, usually without doing the arithmetic out loud, that the odds of an audit times the cost of an audit come in under the cost of compliance. They might be right. The reserve strategy simply makes the same bet *explicitly*, with the money actually set aside in case they're wrong.

The reserve is useful no matter which position you land on:

- If you and your CPA conclude your facts support **not** paying Mexican tax, the reserve manages the residual uncertainty.
- If you and your CPA conclude payment **is** appropriate, the reserve simply becomes your tax fund.

Either way, each year you calculate the theoretical Mexican liability on your salary (your personal-services income) and set that amount aside in a dedicated U.S. account. Say you earn $150,000 and pay yourself a $70,000 salary through an S-Corp: the theoretical Mexican exposure sits on the $70,000. At Mexican marginal rates of roughly 30%, that's a maximum reserve near $21,000. Size it to the worst plausible interpretation, not the best case.

Three ways it can play out:

- **Never audited by SAT.** The reserve grows in your account. After you've left Mexico permanently and time passes, practical risk approaches zero and the reserve is simply money you kept.
- **Audited, SAT agrees with your position.** Same outcome. The money is yours.
- **Audited, SAT disagrees.** You have the funds to pay without a crisis. You're negotiating from preparedness instead of panic.

One legal note: Mexico's statute of limitations runs five years from the filing deadline. If you never filed a Mexican return, SAT's position is that the clock never started. In practice, collecting on old years from someone who has already left Mexico with no Mexican assets is very difficult — but "difficult to collect" and "not owed" are, again, different statements.

## A worked example

Here's how one reader's facts might be analyzed under the configuration above. They spend about 25 days a year in the U.S. and the other 340 in Mexico. They own a U.S. S-Corp with exclusively U.S. clients, and the business runs primarily on U.S. infrastructure, U.S.-based contractors and systems, and U.S. client relationships.

The numbers: total income $150,000. S-Corp salary $70,000. Distributions of $80,000, which under Article 7's permanent-establishment test carry no Mexican exposure. The theoretical Mexican exposure on the *salary* depends entirely on how much of that work is attributed to Mexican soil. At the maximum interpretation — 100% of the salary attributed to Mexico — the theoretical tax at roughly 30% is about $21,000. Where the work is documented as concentrated during U.S. visits, the exposure is proportionally lower. For context, the overall structure (FEIE, state-tax elimination, cost-of-living reduction) saves this person somewhere between $50,000 and $130,000 a year.

So the annual reserve tops out around $21,000 — roughly 16–20% of the annual savings at the high end. Calculate it, set it aside, invest it.

**The POEM caveat.** Under international tax law, a company's "Place of Effective Management" can independently create a permanent establishment. The hours worked in Mexico should be operational execution, not strategic direction. The strongest support is a formal Officer Delegation of Management Authority, executed *before* you relocate, specifying which decisions live with U.S.-based team members and which the owner handles during U.S.-based governance sessions. The document only works if it describes what actually happens.

**The dual-narrative discipline.** Whatever work pattern you represent has to be consistent across both countries. If the IRS asks whether your salary is reasonable compensation, your answer has to be compatible with what you'd tell SAT about work performed in Mexico. The two narratives don't have to be identical, but they have to be reconcilable.

In one sentence: under this configuration, you are a U.S. tax resident under both Mexican domestic law and the treaty; your S-Corp has no Mexican permanent establishment; distributions are protected under Article 7; and the theoretical exposure on your salary is contained by a reserve sized to the worst plausible interpretation. That's a defensible position — defensible, not guaranteed — and it's defensible precisely because it's documented and reserved for. A position that files and pays in Mexico is equally legitimate; it simply resolves the same uncertainty by paying it rather than reserving against it. Both are real choices. Neither is the one this book makes for you.

## What failure looks like

SAT cross-references immigration entry/exit records against its database. A foreign national who shows up in Mexico 300-plus days a year with no RFC (tax ID) and no filings is not currently a priority. But *if* flagged, the sequence goes like this: SAT sends a request for documentation. You're asked to show either that you're not a Mexican tax resident or that your income is exempt under the treaty. If you have a U.S. dwelling lease, tax returns, contracts, and travel records, you respond with a prepared defense. If you don't, you're assembling one retroactively, under pressure — always the weaker place to be.

This is why the "nobody files" consensus has held so long. The people telling you not to bother aren't lying about their experience; they genuinely haven't been audited, and statistically most won't be. But "low probability" and "zero exposure" aren't the same thing, and the gap between them is exactly where the reserve strategy lives. Operating without a reserve isn't being wrong about *today's* enforcement — it's being exposed to a change in that environment tomorrow, or to the small but nonzero chance your own situation gets flagged.

Without the delegation document and evidence of U.S.-based operational execution, SAT can attribute your full working time to your Mexican presence. That pushes the theoretical exposure to the full ~$21,000 a year on 100% of salary, plus penalties and interest, potentially across several years. With no reserve, that's a five-figure emergency. With a reserve, it's a negotiation. That's the whole case for the reserve, stated without drama: it converts a possible emergency into a manageable line item, whichever position you took.

## The "I only work in the U.S." trap

Here's the most natural temptation in the whole chapter, and a warning about it. If the theoretical Mexican exposure turns on where the labor is physically performed, why not just claim you do 100% of your work during those 25 days back in the States?

To see why that's a mistake, understand what SAT can and can't observe. SAT can verify your physical presence through immigration records — if you spent 340 days in Mexico, they can confirm it. Through the treaty's information-exchange provisions, SAT can request your U.S. tax returns from the IRS, which reveal your W-2, your S-Corp return, your income figures. But a W-2 has no "location of work" field. Nothing on your U.S. returns says where the labor physically happened. And under Mexico's constitutional framework, SAT has no extraterritorial reach — it can't subpoena your email provider, your project-management software, or your U.S. clients.

So SAT can establish that you were *in* Mexico. It generally cannot independently establish what you *did* while there. That gap is what makes the "I only worked in the U.S." claim tempting — and that's exactly what makes it dangerous.

Claiming zero work hours in Mexico blows up the structural integrity of the whole framework. An auditor knows an active business owner drawing a $70,000 salary doesn't go dark for 340 days a year. If SAT asks you to substantiate it, you're forced either to admit the truth (destroying your credibility) or to fabricate evidence (turning a civil matter into fraud). It also creates a fatal mismatch on the U.S. side: if you genuinely only work 25 days a year, the IRS can argue your S-Corp salary is unreasonably low and unwind your U.S. structure.

The honest path prices reality; it doesn't hide it. If you work from Mexico, the framework accounts for it. You document that the heavy lifting — annual planning, core contracts, major client pitches — happens during your U.S. visits, you attribute a realistic, defensible share of your salary to Mexican soil, and you reserve for it. That's a position you can stand behind. The version built on a fiction isn't.

## Options by risk tolerance

There isn't one right answer here — there's a spectrum, and where you land on it is a conversation with your CPA. Three points along that spectrum:

**Option 1 — Stay under 183 days (the cleanest on paper).** Spend no more than 183 days in Mexico in a calendar year *and* no more than 183 in any rolling 12-month period. Both the residency determination and the non-resident income exemption apply cleanly; day 184 triggers Mexican taxing rights on your personal-services income under the treaty. This removes essentially every ambiguity — at the cost of real multi-country logistics.

> A wrinkle your CPA has to track: three different day-count rules run at the same time. Mexican domestic law (CFF Article 9) uses the calendar year. Treaty Articles 14 and 15 use a rolling 12-month period. FEIE's PPT uses *any* rolling 12-month period. Three different calendars, tracked independently.

**Option 2 — Operate through an S-Corp.** If you're self-employed and spending 183-plus days in Mexico, the S-Corp arguably separates salary (exposed) from distributions (protected under Article 7's PE test). This doesn't eliminate exposure; it reduces the portion of income exposed.

**Option 3 — Full-time in Mexico with U.S. infrastructure and a reserve.** The configuration described earlier in this chapter and illustrated in the worked example: no more than 35 days in the U.S., a genuine dwelling, U.S.-source income, S-Corp structure, and a reserve sized to the worst plausible interpretation.

Each option trades something for something else. None of them is labeled "the smart one," because which is smart depends entirely on facts this book can't see.

## Your U.S. dwelling: practical options

A "permanent home" under the treaty means a dwelling available for your continuous use — not a hotel, a short-term rental, or a mail-forwarding address. A private mailbox (PMB) does not qualify. Options, roughly cheapest to strongest:

- **Option A — A family member's home (~$0/month).** A designated room in a parent's or friend's house in your domicile state, documented with a written agreement and photos. The weakest option, but it may satisfy the "abode in another country" threshold.
- **Option B — A nominal room arrangement (~$50–$100/month).** A rented room or standing arrangement in your domicile state. The financial transaction proves it's real. Affordable and defensible.
- **Option C — A dedicated lease (~$600–$1,500/month).** A year-round lease on a studio or apartment. The gold standard, worth considering at higher income levels.
- **Option D — A room in a property you rent out in your no-tax domicile state (~net $0 or cash-flow positive).** If you own property in your no-tax domicile state — Florida, Texas, Nevada, and the like — rent it out while keeping a room for your use during U.S. visits. This is the strongest position: a genuine dwelling by deed and lease, rental income that's U.S.-source, and an active property. **Important:** this only works if the property is in your *no-tax* domicile state. If your only U.S. property is in California, New York, or another high-tax state you used to live in, do **not** keep a room there — it hands that high-tax state a domicile-based argument against your move (see Chapter 3). Rent that property out fully and use A, B, or C for your treaty dwelling.

How to choose: own property in your no-tax domicile state? Option D. Only own property in your former high-tax state? Rent it out fully and use A, B, or C. No U.S. property at all? Option B. High earner? Option C. Family in a no-tax domicile state? Option A.

## Three reader profiles: where do you stand?

**Profile 1 — Clear non-residency.** If most or all of these describe you, a non-residency position is on strong footing:

- 100% U.S.-source income
- U.S. clients or employer
- All income deposited to U.S. accounts
- A genuine U.S. dwelling maintained
- Registered to vote and licensed in your domicile state
- No RFC, no Mexican invoices
- No Mexican accounts receiving income
- Periodic U.S. visits

Here the treaty tends to resolve in your favor. Salary/personal-services income still faces the 183-day analysis, managed by the reserve strategy.

**Profile 2 — The cautious optimizer.** Same facts as Profile 1, plus you spend no more than 183 days in Mexico per calendar year. Day 184 triggers Mexican taxing rights on your personal-services income; below that threshold, treaty exemptions apply cleanly, with no theoretical exposure on any income stream.

**Profile 3 — The gray zone.** The more of these that describe you, the weaker a non-residency position — and the single biggest factor is whether you maintain a genuine U.S. dwelling:

- Only a PMB in the U.S. (no actual dwelling)
- Lived in Mexico 300+ days/year for 3+ years
- Children in Mexican school
- A long-term lease on, or ownership of, a Mexican home
- Mexican rental property (any income at all)
- A Mexican bank account receiving rental or other income
- No U.S. visit in over a year

Without a U.S. dwelling, the analysis never even reaches the tests where your U.S.-source income could carry the position. Get individualized guidance before assuming anything.

## The bottom line

You can live in Mexico and be fully compliant with both governments. What that compliance *looks like* — whether it includes Mexican income tax, a reserve against the possibility, or a life structured to stay under 183 days — depends on facts only you and your CPA can weigh. There's a version of a clean, defensible life on more than one of these paths.

The full-time configuration in this chapter — U.S. economic infrastructure, a genuine dwelling, and a prudent reserve while living in Mexico — rests on strict adherence to the letter of the treaty. It demands excellent documentation and an honest accounting of your actual work hours. Done that way, it lets you treat cross-border taxation like a predictable business risk instead of a recurring emergency. A path that files and pays in Mexico rests on the same virtues — documentation, honesty, professional guidance — and simply resolves the uncertainty by paying it. Both are legitimate. Both are defensible. Neither is the one we're choosing for you.

Many expats will keep doing what they've always done: nothing. That's worked for years and may keep working. If it does, they'll have been right. If enforcement tightens or their own situation gets flagged, they'll be building a defense from scratch, under pressure, with no reserve and no documentation. The framework here isn't an argument that they're wrong about the present. It's a reminder that being right about the present isn't the same as being prepared for the future — and that preparation is available to you regardless of which position you take.

**So here is the one thing this chapter does tell you to do:** whether you pay Mexican taxes, reserve for the possibility, or arrange your life to stay under 183 days, make it a decision you reach *with your CPA, based on your specific facts* — not one you absorb by default from the person next door. Structure determines the outcome. Documentation defends it. A reserve prices the uncertainty. And an honest read of where your facts actually place you is the foundation under all of it.

### Your next action

You don't need to resolve your Mexican tax position this week. You need to stop leaving it unexamined. Three moves:

1. **Book a consultation with a CPA who does cross-border U.S.–Mexico work** — not your old hometown accountant. This is a specialty.
2. **Start the document folder now** — tax returns, contracts, lease, voter registration, license, travel records. The defense you might one day need is built quietly, in advance, not retroactively.
3. **Decide on purpose.** Whatever position you and your advisor choose, choose it. Inheriting one by accident is the only genuinely wrong move in this chapter.

Mark eventually did exactly that. He sat down with a cross-border specialist, established a Florida dwelling, visited it each year, documented his U.S. economic ties, structured his income through an S-Corp, and set aside a calculated reserve. He stopped waking up at 2 a.m. — not because the uncertainty vanished, but because he'd finally priced it and written it down. His neighbor, working from her accountant's reading of her own facts, files and pays in Mexico, and sleeps fine too. They started from similar circumstances and, with their advisors, reached different defensible answers. That's the honest shape of this decision: not a right answer and a wrong one, but a real choice — and the only mistake is making it in your sleep.
