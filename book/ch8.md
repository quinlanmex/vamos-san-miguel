# Chapter 8: Preparing to Move

## The driveway

The garage sale stretched across our entire driveway and half the lawn. Six years of post-Argentina accumulation spread out under Jacksonville's August sun. An espresso maker we had used twice. Bikes that had gotten plenty of use but would not fit our new life. Boxes of books we would never re-read. Our neighbors wandered through, picking up pieces of our existence with careful reverence.

"Twenty dollars," I told Mrs. Henderson when she asked about the espresso maker. Her face lit up like she had discovered buried treasure.

We had done this before. The Buenos Aires departure taught us the mechanics. Six years back in the States had also taught us how quickly Americans re-accumulate, even after swearing we had learned our lesson. The espresso maker was a small monument to that amnesia, purchased during some optimistic Sunday-morning fantasy that never materialized.

This second round carried different weight. Every item we priced became a small act of liberation, each decision carving away another tether to patterns we had unconsciously rebuilt. The teenager who bought our desktop computer for his first gaming setup beamed with the same excitement we had felt browsing rental listings in San Miguel. His father counted out crumpled twenties with obvious pride.

Standing in that empty garage three days later, I understood that preparation asks two different things of you. One is the practical mechanics of an international move, which you can learn from a checklist. The other is the psychological work of releasing what you own, which no checklist can do for you. Most of this chapter is the mechanics. The harder half runs underneath all of it.

But before any of that, there is a piece of money most American homeowners refuse to look at directly. The equity sitting in your house will not change your life until you put it to work.

## The paper wealth trap

Your home's value went up 40 percent over the past five years. Congratulations. You are wealthy on paper and standing still in practice.

The trap works like this. Home appreciation only becomes real wealth if you are willing to downsize or relocate to a cheaper market. Sell your $600,000 house that was worth $400,000 five years ago, and if you buy a similar house in the same market that also appreciated 40 percent, you have gained nothing except a larger mortgage at a worse interest rate. The $200,000 "gain" evaporates on the way to the closing table of your next purchase.

The housing market of the mid-2020s makes this sharper than usual. Prices stay high because current owners are locked in. They bought when prices were lower and financed at 3 to 4 percent, and they cannot afford to sell and rebuy at today's rates even when they badly want to move. Federal Housing Finance Agency researchers put numbers on exactly this effect. For every percentage point that market rates sit above a homeowner's original rate, the probability of a sale drops about 18 percent, and the lock-in prevented roughly 1.33 million home sales between mid-2022 and late 2023 while pushing prices up around 5.7 percent through sheer lack of supply.[1] Sales slow, prices hold, and the market feels frozen because in a real sense it is.

Everyone assumes real estate always goes up. Then 2008 happened. By that October, close to 10 million homeowners owed more on their mortgages than their homes were worth, and the count kept climbing into the following years.[2] Some took fifteen years to recover. Some never did.

You are up right now. The market handed you a 40 percent gain, already locked in on paper. The only question is whether you take it while it is there or gamble that it stays.

### "But what about my mortgage interest deduction?"

This objection confuses a tax deduction with actual money. The mortgage interest deduction reduces your taxable income. You are still paying the interest and getting a partial rebate through lower taxes.

Run the numbers. Pay $20,000 a year in mortgage interest in the 24 percent bracket, and the deduction saves you $4,800 in taxes. You are still out $15,200 in interest. The bank got $20,000, the government handed back $4,800, and you called the difference "savings."

Now put that same money to work. Sell the house, invest the $200,000 in equity, and at a long-run market return in the neighborhood of 10 percent (roughly the S&P 500's average since 1957) you earn about $20,660 the first year, with no interest payments eating into it.[3] The deduction you were mourning costs you $15,200 a year to keep, while a Mexico-based life can put you ahead by $35,000 or more annually through lower expenses and investment returns on capital you finally freed up.

The mortgage interest deduction earns its keep when you need housing and can afford the payment. Holding a house specifically for the deduction while better options sit right there is the financial equivalent of buying things you do not need because they are on sale.

Mexico lets you extract that paper wealth and convert it into an actual life. If you are not ready to go all in and want an escape hatch, rent out your U.S. home for roughly twice what a similar place costs you in Mexico. The equity stops being theoretical and starts funding a better life, with no forced downsizing. If you are ready to commit, sell, bank the equity, and rent in Mexico while the capital compounds. A $200,000 gain invested at the assumed return grows to about $326,000 in five years and $533,000 in ten, while your monthly spending falls and your daily life improves.

Your home's appreciation only matters if you can reach it without sacrificing quality of life. That is the whole point of what follows.

## The three phases of strategic preparation

A move to Mexico works when you run three overlapping phases in order, each building the base for the next. Think of it as constructing a house. Phase 1 pours the foundation, Phase 2 frames the structure, Phase 3 installs the finishes. Rush the foundation and everything shifts. Skip the framing and nothing connects. Botch the finish work and you land in Mexico scrambling to handle basics while culture shock is already knocking.

- **Phase 1, Financial and Legal Foundation (3 to 4 months out).** Set up the infrastructure that supports the move: banking, taxes, legal domicile, healthcare. These systems take time to process changes and often need documentation pulled from your current situation.
- **Phase 2, Physical Transition (1 to 2 months out).** Reshape your material life. Decide what stays, what goes, and what travels with you. This phase needs emotional processing time, not just logistics.
- **Phase 3, Final Logistics (2 weeks out).** Handle the time-sensitive tasks that can only happen close to departure: travel bookings, final document checks, last-minute arrangements.

Plan them deliberately and each phase creates momentum for the next.

## Phase 1: building your foundation

### The domicile decision

Before you touch anything else, decide whether changing your legal domicile makes financial sense. Chapter 3 covers the full state domicile change process, but the timing decision affects every other step here.

If you currently live in a state with income tax, establishing residency in a tax-free state before you move to Mexico can save thousands a year on remote-work income. Do this first, before you renew a driver's license, update voter registration, or change bank addresses. The process usually needs two to three months of documented residency establishment, so start early if a domicile change benefits you. Chapter 3 has the detailed requirements and strategies.

### Document renewal timeline

Nothing derails Mexico preparation like discovering your passport expires in three months. Handle renewals early. Government processing times vary unpredictably, and you need these documents for other steps.

| Document | Timing rule |
| --- | --- |
| Passport | Renew if expiring within 18 months. Mexican residency applications often require validity years beyond your application date. |
| Driver's license | Wait until after your domicile change if you are relocating to a tax-free state. |
| Credit cards | Replace any expiring within 12 months. |
| Debit cards | Keep backup cards, stored separately from your primary cards. |

### The backup card strategy

International living runs on redundancy. Order backup credit and debit cards from different banks (Visa, Mastercard, and Amex for credit; three separate institutions for debit if you can), test them before you leave, and store them apart from your primary cards.

Our friend Sarah arrived in Mexico with one credit card. Week two, her bank's fraud algorithm flagged the Mexican transactions and froze the account. No ATM withdrawals, no restaurant payments, complete financial paralysis in a foreign country while the bank "investigated" for four days. She could have prevented the whole ordeal with exactly what you are doing now, which is building redundancy before departure. When you notify your banks of your travel dates before you leave (and you absolutely should), mention that you will be establishing residency abroad. That context heads off the fraud holds that catch most first-time expats.

### Healthcare infrastructure planning

Your healthcare foundation touches every other decision. The Healthcare chapter covers the full strategy for medical care in Mexico. For timing, start early. Insurance changes often take 60 to 90 days to process, and prescription stockpiling works best when you coordinate it with your annual benefit cycle.

## Phase 2: the great unburdening

### Reframing ownership

Americans accumulate objects the way other people buy insurance, as a hedge against imagined futures. The espresso maker stood in for theoretical Sunday mornings. The bikes stood in for fitness we could reconsider abroad. The boxes of books promised leisure re-reading that never came.

Moving to Mexico forces a confrontation with the difference between the possessions that serve you and the possessions you serve. The work gets easier once you change the question from "What if I need this?" to "Does this add value to the life I am building?"

Identity gets stored in objects long after their usefulness runs out. That is the real discovery during preparation. Each possession you pick up is asking a quieter question underneath: which parts of your identity are you ready to grow into, and which have you been postponing out of pure inertia? The espresso maker was never about Sunday mornings. It was about the version of ourselves we kept promising to become. Sort your possessions through that lens and the decisions stop feeling like loss and start feeling like clarity.

### The four-category system

Every item in your home fits one of four categories. Assign ruthlessly and process systematically.

| Category | What it holds | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pack It or Panic | Items you would genuinely regret leaving: key documents, medications, electronics, work equipment, seasonal clothing | 2 to 3 suitcases maximum |
| Profit and Part Ways | Anything worth more than $100: furniture, electronics, vehicles, jewelry, collectibles | Sold individually on Facebook Marketplace, specialized platforms, or in the garage sale |
| Pass It On | Useful but not valuable enough to sell alone: books, clothing, household goods, decor | Local charities, Salvation Army, Goodwill, schools, libraries |
| Toss the Guilt | Kept out of obligation or phantom "what if": expired medications, dead electronics, damaged furniture, documents to shred, junk drawers | Discarded or shredded |

### The Facebook Marketplace reality

Selling our possessions through Facebook Marketplace revealed something I did not expect about our own community. The young father buying our coffee table measured it three times to be sure it fit his apartment. The college student who bought our desk chair sent a thank-you message with a photo of her finished study setup. The retired teacher who bought our bookshelves spent twenty minutes talking about favorite authors.

Behind all the algorithm-fed pessimism about American communities, most people still handle a basic transaction with decency. Every sale turned into a small reminder of shared humanity, and a small transfer of value from objects we had stopped using to people who needed them, funding the life we were choosing instead.

A few tactics that made it work:

- **Photograph everything well.** Natural lighting, clean backgrounds, multiple angles.
- **Price competitively.** Research comparable listings and price at least 10 to 15 percent below market. The closer your move date, the deeper the discount.
- **Bundle similar items.** Groups of books, kitchen items, or decor sell faster than single pieces.
- **Meet in public.** Safe, well-lit locations for anything valuable.
- **Stay flexible on payment.** Venmo, Zelle, and cash all work. Let buyers pick.

### The property decision matrix

Homeowners face Mexico's biggest preparation opportunity and its biggest complexity. Three options, each fitting a different plan and level of commitment.

**Option 1: rental revenue.** Most American homes rent for more than a full Mexico budget. A Jacksonville house renting at $2,800 a month easily covers a $1,500 Mexico budget for an equal or better lifestyle, while you keep building equity and gain a natural hedge against peso swings. Self-management is increasingly workable from abroad. Zillow Rental Manager, Avail, and TurboTenant handle tenant screening, leases, and rent collection. Pair those with local contractor relationships for maintenance, and you can run most situations by video call. Line up your reliable plumber, electrician, HVAC tech, and handyman before you leave, and document the property thoroughly with photos, manuals, and service history. If you would rather be hands-off, full-service property management runs about 8 to 12 percent of rental income and covers marketing, screening, collection, maintenance, and legal compliance. Vet any company carefully, ask for references from other out-of-state owners, and read the fee structure before you sign.

**Option 2: sell for maximum liquidity.** Selling generates the most cash and the cleanest break. It suits families certain about long-term Mexico living or anyone who needs cash for setup costs. Price against recent comparable sales, not optimistic Zillow estimates. An experienced agent typically nets you more than a for-sale-by-owner attempt even after commission. Expect a slow market, roughly 60 to 120 days from listing to closing.

**Option 3: strategic storage.** Keeping the house while you rent in Mexico preserves flexibility if you are testing the move or keeping strong U.S. ties. Do the arithmetic first. An empty house bleeding $2,500 a month in mortgage, taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs $30,000 a year, which is enough to fund two years of Mexico living.

### Vehicle decisions

Your car is one of your largest assets and one of your trickier calls.

- **Sell.** Maximum cash, full commitment. Research value on KBB, Edmunds, and local dealer trade-in offers. Private-party sales through Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, or AutoTrader usually beat a dealer trade-in by 15 to 20 percent, at the cost of more time and effort. We sold our Toyota Highlander through a local CarMax and had an excellent experience. It sold within a week at our asking price.
- **Store.** Preserves flexibility for an uncertain return. Weigh storage cost against depreciation. Climate-controlled storage protects value at $150 to $300 a month. Storing with family or a friend saves money but needs a clear agreement about maintenance, insurance, and access. A stored car needs monthly attention. Someone has to run the engine 15 to 20 minutes, move it to prevent flat spots on the tires, and keep fuel stabilizer in a full tank. Budget $50 to $100 a month if you cannot do it yourself. Neglect it for three to six months and you are potentially looking at thousands in repairs.
- **Rent through Turo.** Generates monthly income while you keep the car. It only works if you have reliable local support for cleaning, maintenance, and customer interaction. Without that, the income disappears under coordination costs.

### What to pack versus what to buy there

Mexico's cost structure inverts American assumptions. Electronics carry import premiums, clothing quality varies widely, and household staples are cheap and everywhere. Understanding those inversions turns packing from generic travel advice into a financial decision.

| Category | Strategy | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electronics | Bring everything | Laptops, tablets, phones, chargers, adapters, work gear (cameras, microphones), e-readers. Import duties and thin retail competition make Mexican replacements a budgeting shock. |
| Clothing | Bring seasonal essentials | Winter layers for mountain towns, professional clothing for video calls, specialized athletic gear (hiking boots, running shoes). Good clothing exists in Mexico but often costs more due to import fees. |
| Kitchen items | Buy there | Pots, pans, and utensils are cheap and available everywhere. |
| Bedding and towels | Buy there | Bulky and heavy to ship, readily available, with mid-range quality in every city. |
| Medications | Bring a 90-day supply | Many drugs are over-the-counter in Mexico. Bring enough to bridge while you find local equivalents. The Healthcare chapter covers this in detail. |
| Documents | Bring originals | Birth and marriage certificates, diplomas, financial records, tax returns (last 3 years), medical and vaccination records. Keep digital copies in cloud storage with the app already installed on your phone. We use Dropbox. |

On the winter layers: if you are heading to the mountains, this is not optional. Snow is nearly nonexistent, but nights in the 30s and 40s Fahrenheit are normal in January even when the afternoon high reaches the 70s.

### Traveling with pets

For families bringing a pet, the process sounds Byzantine and turns out to be mostly paperwork. The full logistics live in the Healthcare chapter. Here are the essentials, illustrated by friends who did it.

Our friends Sarah and Mike brought their 70-pound German Shepherd, Baron, to San Miguel. Watching them work through it taught us that the requirements sound complicated only until you break them into steps.

Mexico's current rules for a dog or cat coming from the U.S. center on a few things: a current rabies vaccination, treatment against internal and external parasites within the six months before entry, and an inspection by SENASICA (Mexico's agricultural health authority) when you arrive. Cats do not need a health certificate. Dogs currently do need one from a USDA-accredited veterinarian, tied to screwworm inspection.[4] These rules have shifted more than once in recent years, so confirm the current SENASICA and USDA requirements before you book, rather than trusting a blog post (including this one) written at a single moment in time.

A few practical points from Baron's trip:

- **Certificate timing.** Sarah's vet handled the exam and paperwork, then submitted it to the local USDA office for endorsement, which took a few business days. They scheduled the vet visit with a week or more of buffer before the flight.
- **Vaccinations.** The rabies vaccination has to be current (given at least 30 days before travel and not expired). The vet verifies everything during the exam.
- **Airline rules.** Each airline sets its own crate size, weight limits, and cabin-versus-cargo policy. Small dogs and cats under roughly 15 to 20 pounds usually travel in-cabin. Larger pets fly as checked baggage or cargo. Baron flew as checked baggage in a climate-controlled hold.
- **Crate training.** They spent three weeks acclimating Baron to the travel crate so the flight would not traumatize him.
- **Day of travel.** Airlines want you there early, typically three to four hours before an international flight. Do not sedate a pet for air travel. The American Veterinary Medical Association advises against it, because sedatives raise the risk of heart and respiratory trouble at altitude, where their effects are poorly understood and no one can monitor your animal mid-flight.[5] Sarah fed Baron lightly four to six hours out and offered water until two hours before departure.
- **Arrival.** Mexican customs inspected Baron's documentation in about five minutes, stamped it, and waved them through.

The short version on ongoing care, covered fully in the Healthcare chapter: veterinary care in Mexico is excellent and affordable.

## Common preparation disasters, and how to avoid them

**The $8,000 storage unit.** Our friend Tom could not decide what to do with his stuff, so he put all of it in a storage unit "temporarily." Three years later he has spent $8,000 storing items worth maybe $3,000, has not opened the unit in 18 months, and cannot remember what is in there. Storage should run three months maximum while you sell, or six months maximum for items you will genuinely retrieve. Anything longer is expensive self-deception. Set a hard date to empty the unit and put a calendar reminder on it.

**The expired passport.** Mike realized his passport expired in four months, two weeks before his planned departure. Expedited processing costs extra and adds stress you do not need at that point. Check expiration dates the day you decide to move. Renew anything expiring within 18 months, because Mexican residency applications often require validity well beyond the application date.

**The missing winter coat.** Jessica arrived in San Miguel in August with only shorts, dresses, and t-shirts. San Miguel's January nights run cold, with average lows in the mid-40s Fahrenheit and colder nights dipping toward 42.[6] She replaced the coat she had left in storage with an overpriced, less comfortable one from a tourist shop. Pack one checked bag of "opposite season" clothing. Mexico's mountain colonial cities have real winters despite the tropical latitude. Beach towns stay warm year-round.

## Phase 3: final logistics

### The technology checklist (4 to 6 weeks out)

The Working Remotely and Utilities chapters cover the full cross-border technology setup. The critical pre-departure tasks: test your VPN against every financial service you use, verify international access to your banking, and run complete backups of every device.

### Travel booking strategy (2 to 4 weeks out)

- **Flight flexibility.** Book refundable or changeable tickets when you can. Mexico timelines shift constantly because of document delays, rental arrangements, and surprises. The $100 to $200 premium for flexibility beats an $800 change fee later.
- **Travel insurance.** Coverage for trip interruption, medical emergencies, and baggage runs $100 to $300 and buys real peace of mind. Allianz, Travel Guard, and World Nomads offer different levels. Read the terms closely, because many policies exclude "moving abroad" as a covered reason for changes.
- **Accommodation overlap.** Book your first Mexico housing with arrival flexibility. Flight delays and customs snags derail tight schedules. Airbnb's flexible cancellation works well for the first month while you search for a longer-term rental.
- **Arrival transportation.** Research the trip from airport to lodging before you land. Uber works in the major cities. Smaller towns may need a prearranged taxi. Airport shuttles to popular expat destinations (San Miguel, Puerto Vallarta, Playa del Carmen) offer fixed pricing, and you can almost always book one at the airport when you land.

### Estate planning and digital access

My brother stared at the screen as I walked him through my 1Password vault like we were setting up a Netflix account. "So if something happens to you in Mexico, I just click here for your banking info?"

International living raises the stakes on organized estate planning. Use the move as motivation to finish the organizing you have been putting off. Password managers like 1Password, Bitwarden, and Dashlane let you share access securely with family while keeping individual account control, and family plans allow controlled access to specific accounts without compromising security. Set this up before you leave, because configuring it from Mexico only adds friction. Make sure family members know how to reach your important accounts, insurance policies, investment information, and legal documents, and leave a simple shared "In Case of Emergency" document with instructions. International living can slow communication during a crisis, and that one document removes a lot of fear.

## The letting-go breakthrough

Three weeks into preparation, I found myself in the closet staring at seven photo albums and four yearbooks that had followed us through every move for twenty-five years. Wedding photos, vacation snapshots, college memories, childhood documentation, the physical archive of a life lived before smartphones.

When had I last opened these albums? Maybe twice in a decade. Once to show old friends our wedding photos, once during a nostalgic evening that lasted about ten minutes. They demanded storage space, moving logistics, and emotional weight while doing almost nothing for my actual life.

The question that finally landed: am I preserving memories or hauling around guilt?

Every photo captured a moment worth capturing, but the albums themselves had become an obligation. I spent an afternoon selecting maybe thirty images that genuinely mattered, our wedding kiss, family gatherings, the real ones, and scanned them into digital form. I let the rest go. Releasing them felt like shedding fifty pounds of invisible weight. I have not thought about those discarded photos since, except right now while writing this, which is exactly the proof that they had become storage burdens rather than treasures.

The same test transformed every decision after that. College textbooks, yearbooks, exercise equipment, decorative items, all of it faced one question: does this serve the life I am building, or some phantom obligation to preserve every trace of who I used to be? Preparation shows you which possessions matter and which only occupy space. The liberation comes from owning intentionally. You keep what serves the life you are actively building, and you let go of the life you theoretically might live someday.

## Your smooth departure blueprint

Most families need three to four months from decision to departure. Compress it much faster and you miss critical steps. Stretch it much longer and you lose momentum. Four checkpoints carry the weight.

| Checkpoint | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| 3 to 4 months out (legal foundation) | Start the state domicile change if it helps you (Chapter 3). Renew passports for the whole family. Order backup cards from different banks. Decide whether to sell or rent your home. Begin the four-category sort. |
| 6 to 8 weeks out (physical transition) | Request 90-day prescription supplies. Sell the vehicle or arrange storage. Finish Facebook Marketplace sales. Schedule donation pickups. Confirm insurance coverage changes. |
| 2 to 4 weeks out (final systems) | Set up and test your VPN against banking and work systems. Book flights and first-month housing. Notify all banks of travel dates. Back up every device. Arrange mail forwarding. |
| Final week (departure prep) | Reconfirm all travel arrangements. Install WhatsApp and translation apps. Pack and weigh checked bags. Make physical and digital document copies. |

## When preparation reveals you are not ready

Three months into sorting possessions, our friend Rachel called. "I can't do this," she said. "Every box I pack feels wrong. I keep crying over random kitchen items."

She had started with real intention and discovered something valuable, which is that she was not ready. Her aging parents needed her nearby. Her business required a physical presence she had not accounted for. The emotional weight of releasing possessions surfaced attachments she had not recognized in herself.

Rachel did not fail. She learned crucial information before burning bridges and buying one-way tickets. If preparation reveals that you are not ready, financially or emotionally or logistically, that discovery saves you from an expensive mistake. Better to learn it while sorting books in your living room than while panicking in a Mexican apartment with no support system and shrinking savings.

Preparation works both directions. Some people find they are more ready than they thought. Others find they need more time, more money, or different circumstances. Both discoveries have equal value. Moving abroad is an optional financial and lifestyle optimization, not a moral test, and the right timeline is the one that supports your actual life. Some families execute in twelve weeks. Others need eighteen months to build savings or wait for a school year to end. Neither is wrong.

## The psychology of preparation

Preparation is where the financial and psychological advantages first compound, before the taxes change, before the rent drops, before daily life improves. Every completed task builds momentum while cutting the last-minute stress that derails so many moves.

The attachment-release process shows you which possessions actually serve your goals and which serve emotional needs that experiences and relationships meet better. Every object you release opens space for an experience you will gain. Every system you organize today buys security for an adventure tomorrow.

Remember the compound advantage from Chapter 1: every month of delay costs up to $12,375 in potential savings. Preparation can feel overwhelming, but the alternative costs far more. Staying put while your paper wealth sits locked in home equity, and month after month of savings potential quietly disappears, is the expensive choice dressed up as the safe one. The transformation starts long before you board the plane, and the clarity you find in preparation becomes the foundation for everything waiting on the other side.

### Your next action

You do not need to sell the house or book the flight this week. You need to start the two clocks that take the longest to run. Three moves:

1. **Check every expiration date today.** Passports, driver's licenses, credit and debit cards. Renew anything expiring within 18 months, and confirm whether a domicile change (Chapter 3) should come first.
2. **Run the paper-wealth math on your home.** Write down your equity, your annual mortgage interest, and what the house would rent for. Decide, on paper, whether you are a sell, rent, or store case before emotion gets a vote.
3. **Begin the four-category sort in one room.** Not the whole house. One room. Assign every item to Pack It or Panic, Profit and Part Ways, Pass It On, or Toss the Guilt, and notice which decisions are hard. That is the psychological work starting, and it is better to meet it now than at the airport.
