# Chapter 18: Utilities

## The night the house went dark

The neighbors' windows glowed warm and gold across the cobblestone street, the way they do in the tourism brochure. Ours were black. No router blinking, no refrigerator humming, no ceiling fan pushing the late-afternoon heat around. Just me on the tile floor, watching my phone battery drain, wondering whether the expat dream ends this way. Not with a visa rejection or a dramatic culture clash, but with a grown man who cannot keep his own lights on.

"Did you pay the electric bill?" my wife asked from the dark kitchen.

"What electric bill?" I said, which answered the question better than any explanation could have.

Three months in Mexico, quietly proud of how cheap the electricity seemed, and I had never seen a statement, an invoice, or a reminder. Some American reflex told me no news was good news, that Mexican utilities ran on the same invisible autopilot I grew up with. The CFE, Mexico's federal electricity commission, had never agreed to that arrangement.

Here is what that night was teaching me, and what this chapter is for. Mexican utilities work, and they work well, but they run on a logic you have to learn on purpose. Nobody mails you the manual. Learn the logic once and your electricity, gas, water, and internet turn boring in the best possible way. Miss it and you end up on the floor in the dark. This chapter hands you the logic before you hit the floor.

## Electricity: the CFE and the bill that blows away

My landlord laughed when I called him in a barely controlled panic. "Ah, sí, they leave the bill in your door. Sometimes it blows away." He had clearly watched a lot of expats learn this the hard way. "But you can use the CFE app now," he added, like it was an afterthought.

An app. I downloaded it over my phone's hotspot, already throttled because I had blown through my data that week, and watched it crawl in with the pacing of dial-up nostalgia. Registration wanted my address in a format I did not recognize, because Mexican addresses do not follow the street-number-then-city pattern burned into American muscle memory. After three tries and a few Google translations of "Colonia," it accepted where I lived.

Then the billing history loaded, months of it, looking like an indictment. We owed money. We had also, it turned out, been paying reasonable rates on a tiered structure that rewards low consumption. The lowest block of kilowatt-hours costs a fraction of the higher tiers, and the price climbs steeply as you use more [1]. Once I understood the logic, the structure read less like a punishment and more like a nudge toward conservation.

Payment revealed the next layer. Without a local bank account I could not pay through the app itself, but I could carry my phone into any Oxxo convenience store, show the cashier the barcode on the screen, and hand over cash. In a pinch, a trusted Mexican friend lets me use his credit card. Getting your own Mexican credit card runs through the tax-residency requirements covered in the Mexican Taxes chapter, so plan on the Oxxo route at first. High-tech billing, neighborhood-level payment. It works flawlessly once you stop expecting it to look American.

CFE promised power back within 48 hours and delivered the same day.

**Your first week with the CFE.** Download the app on arrival. Register your exact address using an old bill, which your landlord can usually dig up. Set a weekly reminder to check your balance, because the paper bill really does blow away. Then locate the nearest Oxxo, pharmacy, or shop that takes CFE payments, so the first time you owe money you already know where to go.

### When the grid goes dark, and it will

Perfect payment habits will not stop the next thing, which is the outage. Mexico's grid runs differently from the redundant systems common across most of the United States. The country's range of geography, from tropical coast to mountain, leaves more of the network exposed to weather, and older infrastructure in many areas needs more frequent maintenance. Your Mexican neighbors treat a brief interruption as a minor inconvenience rather than a crisis, and after a while, so will you.

The lesson arrived for me three minutes into a client Zoom, when the lights flickered off and left me staring at a dead screen while my colleagues waited in digital limbo. My neighbor turned up at the fence a few minutes later with a battery radio and a knowing smile. "Una hora, más o menos," he said, and pulled up a chair to wait it out.

That afternoon sold me on redundancy. A UPS, an uninterruptible power supply, keeps your modem and router alive through a short outage. A basic unit like the APC Back-UPS Pro 1000VA will hold a modem and router for several hours. For longer blackouts, especially in rural areas, a lithium power station such as UGreen's units can keep you connected for a full day or more. Just as important, a UPS smooths the voltage during Mexico's frequent brownouts, the brief dips that will otherwise crash your whole router stack and force a reboot at the worst moment.

## Internet: the digital lifeline

TelMex covers most of the country for home internet, alongside Totalplay, Izzi Telecom, and Megacable, and the service is often faster and steadier than what many of us had in American cities. The trick is to route installation and repairs through your landlord. That leans on established relationships with local technicians who fix things in hours, and it sidesteps the language barrier at the same time.

TelMex has its own failure modes. During our first month in Puerto Vallarta the internet vanished on a Friday morning. The truck showed up that afternoon, not to reconnect us, but to cut the line entirely. The technician, a cheerful man named Raúl whose English beat my Spanish, explained that our landlord's account for a different property had fallen behind. The collection system had flagged all of his addresses, and Raúl was following orders. "You seem nice," he said, coiling our cable, "but I cannot make exceptions." We spent two days offline over payments that had nothing to do with our rental.

So confirm your internet account status independently, especially in your first month. Ask your landlord for explicit confirmation that the account tied to your unit is current and in good standing. Silence is not the same as stability.

For remote workers, Starlink moves from luxury to necessity, particularly in smaller towns where TelMex coverage thins out and repairs take longer. It pays for itself the first time your primary line dies mid-presentation. Getting it running in Mexico, though, involves friction that does not exist back home.

### Starlink: the cross-border reality

Starlink sounds like plug-and-play until you cross a border. The hardware is not the problem. The paperwork and the payment system are.

Here is the part that trips people up. Changing your account's service and shipping address to a country other than the one you signed up in is not supported. Your account is tied to its original billing region. To get service in Mexico you transfer the hardware onto a new account with a Mexican service address, and that new account requires a new email address [4]. The dish itself does not care where it lives. The billing system cares a great deal. Starlink's policies also shift by region and change often, so check their current terms before betting on any specific workaround.

I waited until I arrived to order, figuring it would be simpler to buy locally and plug in. It became its own small adventure. Ordering from the Mexican Starlink site worked fine until checkout, where it demanded a Mexican billing method and delivery address. Every one of my U.S. cards failed, even with a Mexican address on the account.

A friend in Mexico City placed the order on his Mexican card. I sent him the peso equivalent through Wise, he checked out, and the box arrived within a week. Once I entered my own account credentials the hardware linked instantly and everything worked. Without his card, I would have been stuck.

The lesson: buying Starlink in Mexico, you will probably need a Mexican credit or debit card for the initial setup and first payment. U.S. cards almost always fail at activation now. No Mexican card yet? Ask a trusted friend, landlord, or property manager to place the order, then reimburse them through Wise or bank transfer, or fold it into your rent. You can sometimes switch the payment method to a U.S. card afterward, but it is unreliable, so I still run monthly payments on my friend's card with an automatic Wise transfer to him, plus a little extra to cover the trouble.

**Buy in the U.S. or in Mexico?** Buying in the U.S. and carrying it across still works, with trade-offs. The kits often cost less up front and you can test them before you move, but you have to cancel the U.S. account and reactivate in Mexico, a process complicated by payment failures and possible import delays. A Mexican purchase ships with the correct billing region and no transfer needed, though it usually runs about $100 USD more than U.S. pricing. If you already own U.S. hardware, keep it. You just disassociate the dish from the old account and link it to your new Mexican one after activating service.

**Timing.** Delivery in Mexico typically takes five to ten business days, and activation is nearly instant once the dish powers up. The real delay is securing that first Mexican payment method, so plan on one to two weeks of setup lag before you have steady Starlink. Carry a local SIM as a backup for those early days.

**The community shortcut.** Expats have turned this into a ritual. Post in your local Facebook group before you arrive: "Moving to [city] next month, looking for someone with a Mexican card who can help me order Starlink, happy to buy you dinner." You will get replies within hours from people who have been through it. Budget $100 to $200 USD for a thank-you meal or tip. Consider it part of the initiation.

### Redundant internet: the unbreakable home office

You may not need any of this. We have gone months at a stretch with no connection trouble at all. Then a storm hits, or a car takes out a pole, and sometimes the problem is only your block and you can decamp to a coffee shop for the afternoon. If that is your tolerance, skip ahead. If you want a connection that does not drop during a live meeting, keep reading.

A modern home office does not need the fastest internet in town. It needs internet that does not disappear. The enemy is instability: fiber cuts, ISP outages, Starlink dropouts, voltage dips from CFE, the micro-outages that kill a Zoom call in an instant.

The fix is a failover architecture, an enterprise-grade idea that scales down cleanly to a spare bedroom. At its center sits a dual-WAN router, which accepts two separate internet feeds, typically your local ISP on one port and Starlink on the other. Rather than trusting a single path, the router constantly tests each connection with small health-check probes to services like 1.1.1.1 or 8.8.8.8. When the primary drops, even for a second, the router shifts all traffic to the backup.

Basic failover keeps your browser tabs alive but still interrupts a live Zoom or WhatsApp call, because real-time sessions break when the path changes. To erase even that interruption you add a bonded tunnel, usually through a subscription service like Peplink's SpeedFusion Cloud. The router builds an encrypted tunnel that spans both connections at once, and your call rides inside it, so a dead primary link hands off to the secondary without the call noticing. No reconnection, no freeze.

None of it survives a power cut, so the whole stack sits on a UPS that smooths voltage and keeps the router, the Starlink power injector, and the ISP modem running through a brownout. Without the UPS, a two-second dip crashes everything and forces a reboot.

The complete setup:

| Component | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Primary ISP into WAN1 on a dual-WAN router | Your main connection |
| Starlink (bypass mode plus Ethernet adapter) into WAN2 | The backup path |
| SpeedFusion or equivalent bonding service | Creates the seamless, no-drop tunnel |
| UPS | Keeps every component alive through power flickers |
| Wi-Fi and Ethernet network | Your devices connect to the router as usual |

Configured correctly, this behaves like a single connection that will not break. You can pull the cable from the primary ISP mid-meeting and never lose the call. For a home office abroad, where grid instability is normal, that is the difference between hoping your internet holds and knowing it will.

## Natural gas: the twenty-pound rule

Mexican homes run on propane from large tanks, usually mounted in a courtyard or on the roof. The pressure drops slowly until it hits 20 PSI, then vanishes almost at once. When the gauge reads 20 PSI, order that day. You may have hours, not days.

Your landlord gives you the WhatsApp contact for your area's gas company. Send them a Google Maps pin of your location and they arrive within hours with a full tank. Budget around 2,000 pesos (about $100 USD) for a complete replacement. Everything runs in cash, with no monthly billing and no corporate phone tree. You text a person, they bring gas, you pay them directly. Over time the drivers learn your address, remember your preferences, and sometimes call ahead when they are already in the neighborhood.

Check the hose connections once a year for cracks or brittleness, and shut the tank valve if you will be away for a stretch. It is a small habit that removes a real risk and trims your gas bill at the same time.

This direct relationship strips out the bureaucratic friction of American utility calls. No automated menu, no hold music, no ticket number. Someone you know shows up and solves the problem, and that personal accountability often beats what we were used to in the States.

## Water: safety and scarcity

The water bill comes bundled into your rent, so most landlords handle it directly and you never think about the invoice.

Safety is where you pay attention. Unless your rental has reverse-osmosis filtration, treat the tap water as not safe to drink. Garrafones, the 20-liter jugs, become part of the weekly rhythm at 30 to 40 pesos each, and they guarantee safe drinking water. Many neighborhoods deliver, or you swap empties at a corner store. Some expats install home filtration, though the garrafones win on simplicity with no maintenance.

Depending on where you settle, water scarcity hits many regions during the dry season, much like the American Southwest. Conservation here is both environmentally responsible and socially considerate. Short showers and minimal lawn watering keep resources available for the whole neighborhood, your Mexican neighbors understand the stakes, and respecting the limit builds real social capital.

## Solar power: the numbers that actually pay off

You can skip this section if you do not plan to own a home in Mexico.

For homeowners, solar is one of the few upgrades that reliably delivers both monthly savings and resale value. CFE's tiered pricing makes electricity expensive at the top, and it turns punishing once you cross into DAC rates, the Doméstica de Alto Consumo classification triggered when your rolling 12-month average consumption exceeds your zone's cap [1]. A properly sized, grid-tied array erases most of that exposure.

A five to seven kilowatt system in central Mexico typically pays for itself in about 3.5 to 5 years, depending on consumption and roof orientation, and the case is strongest for households that would otherwise sit in DAC pricing [3]. Once you interconnect under Medición Neta (net metering), excess daytime production is credited against nighttime use, which can flatten your bill down to near the fixed service charge [2]. Over a 20 to 25 year panel lifespan, the cumulative return for these households often lands in the range of 150 to 250 percent, even without batteries.

Batteries are a lifestyle upgrade rather than a financial one. A five to ten kilowatt-hour lithium battery buys outage protection and smoother load management, but it adds little to the return. Batteries earn their keep if you work from home, lean hard on electronics, or simply want resilience where the power is inconsistent. They do not meaningfully speed up payback.

On architecture, microinverters like Hoymiles or Enphase give the best mix of bill reduction, resale value, and reliability, with one trade-off: they provide no backup power during an outage. Hybrid inverters with batteries (Solis, Growatt, Sofar) add blackout protection but cost considerably more and need longer installation planning.

Homes with documented, legally interconnected solar tend to see a modest value lift, driven by lower operating costs and buyer confidence in stable bills. Batteries add emotional appeal, and add resale value only if they are relatively new.

The play: install enough capacity to erase your typical CFE bill, finish all the CFE interconnection paperwork, and favor long-term reliability over peak output on paper. Solar is one of the rare utility decisions where the financially rational choice and the lifestyle-improving choice happen to be the same one.

## Your first-month setup checklist

### Week 1: essential foundation

- Download and configure the CFE app, register your exact address, and set weekly balance reminders.
- Get the gas company's WhatsApp contact, save it prominently, and learn to read the tank gauge.
- Locate your nearest Oxxo or pharmacy and map where you can pay CFE bills.
- Test your internet speeds, verify upload performance for video calls, and confirm the TelMex account status independently.

### Week 2: backup systems

- Install a UPS for your modem and router, the minimum protection during outages.
- Research Starlink as a backup for spotty coverage or a remote location.
- Establish water delivery, setting up garrafón service or finding an exchange point.
- Gather service contacts and billing access, including technician numbers, and confirm account arrangements with your landlord.

### Week 3: system testing

- Run a trial CFE payment at Oxxo before you actually owe money.
- Test your internet failover, if you configured one, and confirm the backup kicks in on its own.
- Verify every utility contact and payment method while you are calm and organized, not mid-crisis.

## Full circle

Six months later I was working late when the familiar darkness rolled across the neighborhood again. This time the house kept humming on backup power while neighbors reached for flashlights. The refrigerator cycled, the router blinked its steady green, and my laptop kept charging. Across the street my neighbor's windows glowed warm and gold in the dark. This time, so did mine.

Mexico does not punish ignorance so much as decline to cushion you from it. CFE does not chase expats who miss payments, the power simply goes off. TelMex does not send reminder emails, the internet just stops. The gas company does not mail collection notices, you just run out of hot water. That absence of a corporate safety net is oddly freeing once you accept the premise, because competence stops being optional and becomes the obvious path. Learn how these systems run, the app, the WhatsApp contact, the gauge, the account confirmation, and you are not fighting bureaucratic friction. You are learning the actual machinery that runs the country, and that competence compounds faster here than it did back home.

### Your next action

You do not need to master every utility this week. You need to stop assuming they will run themselves. Three moves:

1. **Install the CFE app and run a practice payment at Oxxo before you owe a peso.** The trial run turns a future emergency into a routine errand.
2. **Confirm your internet account is current and in your unit's name, in writing, from your landlord.** Then decide whether a UPS and a Starlink backup fit how you work.
3. **Save your gas company's WhatsApp contact and learn the 20 PSI rule now,** so the first time the pressure drops you already know who to text.
