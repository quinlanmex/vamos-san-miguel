# Chapter 20: Shopping & Delivery

## The lime avalanche

My first week in San Miguel, I walked out of La Comer juggling a week of groceries with no bags. The cashier had asked, "¿Quiere bolsa?" and I had smiled and said, "Sí." Then she handed me one woven cloth bag, singular, and I understood my mistake a beat too late. One bag. For a week of food.

I loaded what I could into it and cradled the rest against my chest. Three steps into the parking lot, physics intervened. The top of the pile tipped, and limes scattered across the asphalt like dropped marbles. A container of yogurt made a break for freedom under a parked car. My tortillas fluttered off in the breeze.

Five señoras materialized out of nowhere, laughing and scooping up my runaway produce. They were not laughing at me exactly. They were laughing with recognition, because they had watched gringos do this before. One of them pressed a sturdy mesh bag into my palm. "Para la próxima vez," she said, with a wink.

That parking lot taught me the first rule of shopping in Mexico. Preparation beats convenience. Disposable bags, plastic and paper both, are banned across much of the country, starting with Mexico City on January 1, 2020 [1]. You bring your own or you buy new ones at the register. The cashier had given me precisely what I asked for. The system assumed I would show up ready, and I had not.

This chapter is the version of that lesson you get to read before your own limes hit the pavement. It covers how the retail layers stack up, where the real savings hide, how delivery actually works when the address is "the house with the green gate," and the handful of account and payment quirks that trip up every newcomer. None of it is complicated. It just runs on different assumptions than the shopping you are used to.

## Rewiring how you shop

In the U.S., retail has been engineered to remove friction and, with it, most human contact. Self-checkout, tap to pay, same-day delivery. The goal is to get you in and out without speaking to anyone.

Mexico's more gentrified towns offer plenty of that. La Comer and Costco will feel like home. But step into a traditional store, a stand, or a market and you step back a few decades. You will wait behind someone who is catching the cashier up on her daughter's quinceañera. Your first reaction will be impatience. Give it a few months and you will start to see what you are actually watching, which is commerce that builds community instead of dissolving it.

When your turn comes, you pay in cash, you bag your own items, and you take your time. You wish the other shoppers "buenos días." The American fight-or-flight speed of a grocery run has no place here, and after a while you stop missing it.

## What costs what

Mexican pricing looks random until you learn the two forces behind it: local abundance and the cost of distance.

Local abundance drives the savings. In my experience, fresh produce, meat, dairy, and tortillas run dramatically cheaper than their U.S. equivalents, often well over half off. A family can knock a serious amount off the monthly grocery bill while eating fresher, higher-quality food. The avocados that cost $2 each in Colorado sell for around 50 cents here, and they taste like they were picked that morning, because they usually were.

Imports carry the burden of distance and tariffs. Electronics, clothing, and packaged goods from the U.S. or Asia often cost more than they do back home. An iPhone case that runs $15 on Amazon in the States might be $25 here. The move is obvious once you see it. Stock up on imports during U.S. visits, buy local for daily needs.

Quality follows its own logic, and it splits by source.

| Category | How it compares to the U.S. |
| --- | --- |
| Local unpackaged goods (eggs, produce, meat, tortillas) | Often better. Deeper yolks, tomatoes that taste like tomatoes, meat from animals that lived better lives. |
| Mexican-made packaged goods (paper towels, plastic bags, cereal) | Often flimsier. Towels that shred, bags that tear, cereals loaded with sugar. |
| Imported packaged goods | Familiar quality, higher price. |

The rhythm that falls out of this: lean into local abundance, plan around imports, and set your quality expectations by where a thing came from.

## The shopping ecosystem: finding your circuit

Mexican retail runs in layers, each one built for a different need and price point. Learn the layers and you can assemble a routine that fits your life.

### The international layer

Start here, because this is the layer that will feel like home, and on a hard week that familiarity is worth something. It is also the layer that lulls you into never leaving it, so treat it as a floor, not a ceiling.

**Costco** operates in the major cities, including Mexico City, Querétaro, Guadalajara, Monterrey, Cancún, and Puebla. Same bulk philosophy, tuned for Mexican tastes, familiar brands next to local favorites. The samples lady still works the aisles, and the $5 rotisserie chicken is still the size of a small turkey. Our kids clocked that chicken within a week of arriving and have treated a Costco run as an event ever since.

**Walmart** dominates Mexican retail more thoroughly than it ever managed in the States. It runs several formats: full Walmart Supercenters in cities, smaller Bodega Aurrerá stores in neighborhoods, and Walmart Express for quick trips. The produce sections often beat their American counterparts, with local suppliers keeping them fresh and cheap. The first time I filled a cart there I kept waiting for the prices to catch up to the familiar logo. They never did.

**Sam's Club** reaches further than Costco into smaller cities. Same warehouse-membership model, with a product mix that leans harder on Mexican brands and staples like 20-pound bags of rice and beans.

**Home Depot** shows up in larger cities with U.S.-style hardware shopping, bilingual staff, and familiar tool brands. The lumber section carries tropical hardwoods you will not find up north.

**Office Depot** and **OfficeMax** cover business supplies with familiar layouts and adjusted inventory, more Spanish-language software and different paper sizes.

**7-Eleven** and **Circle K** run the convenience-store beat. Familiar formats, adapted inventory, Slurpee machines next to fresh tortas. They stay open late and often double as safe, well-lit meeting points.

### Department stores

Department stores thrive here in a way American retail abandoned decades ago. The first time we wandered into a Liverpool on a Sunday, I expected the half-dead hush of an American mall and found the opposite: three generations of one family eating together on the top floor, in no hurry to leave. They function as community gathering places, where families spend whole afternoons browsing, eating, and socializing, helped by sales associates who actually have time to help.

| Store | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Liverpool | Mexico's Nordstrom. Upscale fashion, home goods, cosmetics, restaurants under soaring ceilings. |
| Sears Mexico | Vibrant and relevant here, nothing like its American ghost. Everything from wedding dresses to washing machines. |
| Woolworth | Mid-range and unpretentious. School uniforms, work clothes, basic home goods. |
| Suburbia | Trendy fashion at accessible prices, aimed at younger shoppers. |
| Sanborns | In nearly every major mall. Restaurant, bookstore, pharmacy, and gift shop in one. |
| Coppel | Department store crossed with a lender. Furniture, appliances, clothing, electronics, all on pay-over-time plans. |

International fashion fills out the malls: C&A, H&M, Zara, and the rest of the Inditex brands (Bershka, Pull & Bear) clustering together. **Game Planet** is the local GameStop. **Mixup** and **iShop** handle music and Apple gear. **Miniso**, the Japanese-inspired discount chain, covers cheap household goods with better design sense than the American dollar store.

### The national chains

This is the layer where you stop being a tourist and start being a resident, because these are the names you will actually say out loud every week.

**La Comer** is the upscale supermarket, the closest thing to American-style convenience, with wide aisles, imported goods, and an organic section. You pay for the positioning, and some weeks you decide it is worth it.

**Chedraui**, **Soriana**, and other regional chains hold the middle ground. Clean, organized, reasonably priced, good selection of local and imported products. This is where most of our ordinary weeks get shopped.

**Oxxo** owns Mexican convenience retail the way Starbucks owns coffee. The red-and-yellow stores sit on nearly every corner, open 24/7, identical nationwide. They are also neighborhood service centers. You can pay utilities, top up phone credit, receive packages, and send money transfers. The product mix is the same in Polanco and in a small Michoacán village. Within a month the Oxxo on your corner becomes a reflex, the place you drift to at 10 p.m. when you are out of something and out of energy.

For electronics, **Steren** stocks components, cables, and gadgets with staff who understand what they sell, closer to a well-run RadioShack. **Elektra** runs as a rent-to-own electronics superstore with credit plans aimed at working-class families.

### The local networks

**Mini-mercados** dot every neighborhood, perfect for emergency milk, a late-night Coke, or toilet paper at 10 p.m. The owners often live upstairs and know everyone on the block.

**Permanent daily markets** are the heartbeat of food shopping. Individual stalls specialize in produce, meat, cheese, or prepared food. Quality is usually excellent, prices are negotiable, and relationships matter.

**Weekly tianguis**, the traveling street markets, rotate through neighborhoods on set days. You will find perfectly ripe mangoes, bootleg DVDs, and handmade socks in the same three stalls. Part shopping, part entertainment, worth doing at least once for its own sake.

### The digital layer

**Amazon Mexico** works reliably in major cities and gets unpredictable in smaller towns. Depending on where you live, expect roughly 90 to 95% of orders to arrive as promised, with the rest either delayed indefinitely or refunded automatically. In Valle de Bravo I received about nine of every ten items I ordered and got refunds for the stragglers. Prime includes free shipping, though it does not guarantee speed.

**Mercado Libre** is Mexico's dominant e-commerce platform, often faster than Amazon. The catch is payment. Many transactions require a Mexican debit or credit card and a Mexican phone number, which is a real barrier if you have neither.

## The account quirks nobody warns you about

Two setup wrinkles catch almost every newcomer. Neither is a dealbreaker, but both are easier to handle before you need something urgently.

**Amazon and the CURP.** Some newcomers report that Amazon Mexico asks for a CURP, the national ID number you only receive once you have residency. If you are still on a tourist visa and hit that wall, you have workarounds. You can keep shopping the U.S. Amazon site for anything you are willing to bring back or forward, and lean on Mercado Libre or in-person retail for the rest. (Whether the CURP prompt appears seems to vary, so treat this as something to test with your own account. See "To verify.")

**Amazon country switching.** Your Amazon account is tied to a marketplace. To buy physical goods in the States or to order Kindle books on your U.S. account, you switch the account's country in your settings rather than opening a whole new account each time. It is a menu toggle, not a mystery, once you know it lives in your account and content settings.

## The health-conscious circuit

If you eat the way we do, you will not do all your shopping in one place. After a few months most health-focused expats settle into a weekly rhythm that trades a little convenience for a lot of quality and savings.

| Day | Stop | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Fruit and vegetable stand | Best prices and selection, and a chance to practice Spanish with vendors who value your business. |
| Tuesday | Organic or health-food store | Supplements, nut butters, specialty items the supermarkets skip. |
| Wednesday | Major supermarket | Household goods, cleaning supplies, pantry basics. |
| Friday | Costco | Bulk non-perishables, imported brands, the American products you cannot give up. |
| Weekend | Market exploration | New stalls, seasonal specialties, whatever catches your eye. |
| As needed | Amazon | Gaps, hard-to-find items, whatever you forgot. |

## The Mexican label revolution

Mexican food labeling is worth a paragraph on its own, because it is one of the most honest systems anywhere. The law requires black octagonal warning labels on the front of packages: "Exceso de azúcares," "Exceso de sodio," "Exceso de calorías," "Exceso de grasas saturadas." Mexico published this standard, NOM-051, in 2020, and public-health groups have called it among the most advanced front-of-package warning systems in the world [2].

Walking the cereal aisle becomes an education. Nearly every colorful box wears two or three of these stop signs. Meanwhile the produce section sits there label-free, cheaper than the packaged alternatives, and the healthy choice makes itself.

## Delivery: the art of Mexican logistics

Delivery here runs on relationships more than algorithms. The infrastructure exists. It just expects you to meet it halfway.

Address precision matters less than a good description. Bigger towns have the exact street numbers you are used to. Smaller towns and rural spots often have no real address at all, so couriers work from written directions. "Casa con portón verde, frente a la panadería" (house with the green gate, across from the bakery) beats a street number every time.

Local delivery often routes through neighborhood shops or informal pickup points. Your landlord or neighbors will know the local solution. Sometimes a corner store accepts packages for the whole block. Sometimes one house serves as the unofficial neighborhood hub. Some places even offer a dedicated local address for deliveries, so ask your landlord what works where you live.

Relationships speed everything up. Once a driver knows your location, recognizes your face, and trusts that you tip fairly, service improves fast. The package that took five attempts to deliver to a stranger arrives smoothly once you are a regular.

## Paying, and getting things back

**Cards** work widely in supermarkets, department stores, and major chains. **Cash** rules at smaller markets, street vendors, and family shops. ATMs are everywhere, but carry small bills. Breaking a 500-peso note for a 30-peso purchase creates needless drama.

**Loyalty programs** here earn their keep. La Comer, Chedraui, and other chains run points cards that deliver real discounts, built around keeping you as a customer rather than mining your data.

**Return policies** are stricter than the American norm. Keep your receipts, learn the return window before you buy, and do not expect the no-questions-asked returns you took for granted back home. It makes you a more deliberate shopper.

## The alcohol advantage

Mexico's relaxed approach to alcohol erases the confusion of American blue laws. Beer, wine, and liquor sit right in the supermarkets, convenience stores, and corner shops, every day of the week. Local beer runs well past Corona and Dos Equis, with regional craft breweries stocked on ordinary shelves. Wine that would cost $15 to $20 in the States sells for $8 to $12 here, and restaurant markups stay reasonable, which turns a bottle with dinner from a special occasion into a Tuesday.

## Pharmacy culture

Mexican pharmacies function more like small health clinics than retail counters. Many medications that require a prescription in the U.S. can be bought over the counter here, and pharmacists often give basic consultations and recommendations.

Antibiotics are the important exception. Since 2010, Mexican law has required a prescription to buy them, and pharmacies that sell without one face steep fines [3]. Enforcement varies by place and by pharmacy, so you may see the rule applied loosely, but the law is the law and it is worth knowing before you assume otherwise.

Chains like Farmacia Guadalajara, Benavides, and Farmacias Similares run clean, professional operations with knowledgeable staff. Generic medications cost dramatically less than U.S. equivalents, even without insurance. We have had specialty medications ordered and delivered within a week or two, in different parts of the country, more than once. Traditional remedies share the shelves with modern pharmaceuticals, so you will find herbal teas and homeopathic treatments next to the Advil and Pepto-Bismol.

## Essential Spanish for shopping

**At the market**

- "¿Cuánto cuesta?" (How much is this?)
- "¿Me hace precio?" (Can you give me a better price?)
- "Está muy caro." (That's too expensive. Say it with a smile.)
- "¿Más maduro?" (Do you have riper ones?)
- "Me da un kilo de jitomate, por favor." (A kilo of tomatoes, please.)

**At the supermarket**

- "¿Aceptan tarjeta?" (Do you take cards?)
- "¿Dónde está la sección de orgánicos?" (Where's the organic section?)
- "¿Quiere bolsa?" (Do you want a bag? The question that started this chapter.)

**For delivery**

- "Casa con portón negro." (House with the black gate.)
- "¿Me puede marcar cuando llegue?" (Can you call me when you arrive?)
- "Déjelo con el vecino." (Leave it with the neighbor.)

## From efficiency to connection

Six months after my lime avalanche, I ran into one of the señoras who had rescued my groceries. She was shopping at the same La Comer, and she remembered me. We talked about her grandson's school and my slowly improving Spanish. When I reached checkout, I pulled out my own mesh bag, the one she had handed me that first day.

The cashier smiled and asked, "¿Quiere bolsa?" I laughed and held up my bag. "Ya aprendí," I told her. I already learned.

By then I had learned more than bag etiquette. Shopping in Mexico had taught me to slow down, to value the human connections that make a day richer, and to notice the distance my food and goods travel to reach me. The efficiency I lost at the checkout, I got back as belonging. American shopping optimizes for individual convenience. Mexican shopping leans toward social connection and old-world logistics, and after a while you stop wanting to trade one for the other.

Some days you will miss the frictionless ease of Target. Most days you will take Señora Rosa slipping your kid an extra lime at the market instead.

### Your next action

1. **Build your circuit.** Find the best fruit and vegetable stand near you, locate your organic shop and your everyday supermarket, and check whether Costco is close enough to be worth a monthly run. Set up Amazon Mexico now, and Mercado Libre if you have a Mexican card and phone number, before you need something urgently.
2. **Sort out delivery and payment.** Learn your address description in Spanish, ask your landlord about local pickup points or a dedicated delivery address, and start carrying cash and small bills. Keep an empty suitcase ready for imports on your next U.S. trip.
3. **Handle the account quirks before they bite.** Test whether your Amazon Mexico account asks for a CURP, and learn where the country-switching setting lives so you can buy U.S. goods and Kindle books on your U.S. account without a scramble.
