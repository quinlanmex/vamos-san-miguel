# Chapter 23: Other Countries to Consider

## The itch

Two years into San Miguel, you have the rooftop, the fresh juice guy who knows your order, and a residency card that took one appointment and a folder of bank statements to earn. Somebody at the table mentions Da Nang. Beachfront apartment, meals out every night, a motorbike, all of it for eighteen hundred dollars a month. Somebody else brings up a friend in Lisbon. And the question lands the way it always lands, quietly, over the second drink: if Mexico worked this well, where else could I do this?

That itch is the whole point of this chapter. Not because you should leave Mexico. Most people who ask the question stay. The value is in what asking it does to your head. Once you have figured out visas, housing, and daily life in one foreign country, you own a portable skill. You can point it at almost anywhere. Mexico is the training ground, and the training transfers.

So here is the tour. Ten countries ranked by how far the dollar stretches, from the cheapest global havens to the polished European strongholds, plus one special case for the Spanish-averse. For each one, three things worth weighing before you fall in love with a photograph: how cheap it actually is, how close it keeps you to home, and how workable the time zone is if you still earn U.S. income.

A word on the numbers up front. Every cost-of-living figure below is a ballpark, a percentage off typical U.S. spending, useful for sorting the list rather than budgeting your life. The real number depends on your city, your rent, and your habits, which is exactly why the exercise at the end of this chapter asks you to price a stay yourself.

## The countdown

### 1. Vietnam (roughly 70 to 75 percent below U.S. levels)

The cheapest country on the list. Picture eighteen hundred dollars a month covering a modern apartment, every meal eaten out, and a motorbike for the coastal roads. Expat life clusters in Ho Chi Minh City, Hanoi, Da Nang, and Hoi An. The catch is the paperwork. There is no formal retirement visa, so you live on tourist-visa renewals that can turn bureaucratic. The time zone runs twelve to fifteen hours ahead of the U.S., which makes remote work for American clients rough. The lifestyle payoff is large enough that people put up with both.

### 2. Thailand (roughly 65 to 70 percent below)

Southeast Asia's classic expat magnet. Chiang Mai gives you mountain culture, Bangkok gives you a global megacity, and smaller towns like Pai and Hua Hin stay under the radar. A couple lives comfortably on two thousand dollars a month. Visa options are real here: retirement, the long-term resident (LTR), and education visas. Distance from the U.S. is the price of admission. The food, beaches, and healthcare are what you buy with it.

### 3. Colombia (roughly 60 to 65 percent below)

If Asia feels too far, Colombia delivers the same kind of affordability without leaving your time zone. A stylish apartment in Medellín, the "city of eternal spring," runs a fraction of a U.S. equivalent. Coastal Santa Marta mixes Caribbean living with easy reach of coffee country. Colombia's Digital Nomad Visa makes it friendly for remote workers who still bill American clients during American business hours.

### 4. Ecuador (roughly 55 to 60 percent below)

Home to Cuenca's colonial charm and Vilcabamba's so-called "valley of longevity," Ecuador offers one of the cheapest retiree visas on the planet [1]. A couple lives well on sixteen hundred to twenty-two hundred dollars a month, choosing among beach, mountain, and jungle climates. The trade-off shows up in infrastructure, which can feel rough once you leave the major cities.

### 5. Malaysia (roughly 55 to 60 percent below)

Malaysia flies under the radar, and that is part of the appeal. Penang offers island life, Kuala Lumpur offers a cosmopolitan hub, and the Malaysia My Second Home program (MM2H) covers extended stays. Daily costs stay low while healthcare rivals Singapore's for quality. As with Vietnam and Thailand, the time-zone gap with the U.S. makes work calls a chore.

### 6. Mexico (roughly 50 to 55 percent below)

Mexico sits in the sweet spot. Costs run higher than South America and still land dramatically below the U.S. What sets it apart is the support infrastructure: somewhere between 1.5 and 2 million U.S. expats already live here, the largest American population abroad anywhere [2]. Big cities (CDMX, Guadalajara), resort towns (Puerto Vallarta, Cabo), and culture hubs (San Miguel, Oaxaca, Mérida) mean you can design almost any life you want. Proximity keeps visits home cheap, and the temporary-to-permanent residency path has been worn smooth by thousands of Americans a year.

### 7. Panama (roughly 45 to 50 percent below)

Often called Costa Rica's quieter sibling. The Friendly Nations Visa remains one of the simplest residency paths available, though it now runs through a two-year temporary permit before permanent residency and asks for an economic tie such as a job, a business, or a qualifying investment [3]. Boquete is a cool mountain town beloved by retirees; Pedasí is an emerging beach community. Costs sit slightly above Mexico, offset by a stable banking system and the convenience of the U.S. dollar as the currency.

### 8. Costa Rica (roughly 40 to 45 percent below)

Costa Rica sells "pura vida," and the lifestyle carries a premium. Expats gather in Tamarindo for surf and beach or in Central Valley towns like Atenas, where the climate is close to perfect. Budget two thousand eight hundred to three thousand five hundred dollars a month for a couple, higher than Mexico or Panama. In return you get serious biodiversity, strong healthcare, and a long record of political stability.

### 9. Portugal (roughly 25 to 35 percent below)

Europe at a discount. Lisbon and Porto rents have climbed hard, but smaller towns like Évora and Nazaré stay affordable. The D7 and Digital Nomad visas make long stays straightforward, and five years of legal residence opens the door to permanent residency [4]. Costs run above Latin America and still below U.S. coastal cities. The bonus for remote workers is the Atlantic time zone, close enough to the U.S. East Coast to keep a workday functional.

### 10. Spain (roughly 20 to 30 percent below, the most expensive on this list)

The priciest option here, and still cheaper than the U.S. overall. Barcelona, Madrid, and Valencia draw the most expats, while Granada and Cádiz deliver authentic culture at lower cost. Spain launched its Digital Nomad Visa in January 2023 under the Startup Act, one of the more attractive remote-worker packages in Europe [5]. If Mexico is your first step abroad, Spain often becomes the second, in part because you should arrive with a good bit of Spanish already in the tank.

### Special case: Belize (roughly 25 to 35 percent below)

Not as cheap as Ecuador or Colombia, but Belize is English-speaking and close to home. The Qualified Retirement Program (QRP) makes residency simple for retirees [6]. Expats cluster on Ambergris Caye and Placencia. The trade-offs are patchy infrastructure and expensive imports. If Spanish feels daunting, Belize is an easy cultural landing pad.

## What the tour is really for

Mexico stays the best launchpad. It is close, affordable, and familiar, with an expat infrastructure no other country on this list can match. But once you have built the systems to thrive in one foreign country, you see the whole map differently. Whether it is eighteen hundred dollars a month in Da Nang, twenty-two hundred in Medellín, or thirty-two hundred in Lisbon, you will know exactly what you are buying, which is a life you could not have afforded back home.

### Your next action

1. Pick one country from the cheap group (Vietnam, Colombia, or Ecuador) and one from the expensive group (Portugal or Spain).
2. Price out a full six-month stay in each: rent, food, health coverage, flights, and the visa itself.
3. Compare both totals against six months of your current U.S. spending. The gap on the page is the clearest measure you will find of how far your dollar, and your freedom, can stretch.

## To verify

- The cost-of-living percentages for every country are approximations meant for ranking, not budgeting. Price them yourself for your specific city using the six-month exercise above before relying on any single figure.
- U.S. expat headcounts by country (the table's "U.S. expats" column) come from mixed methodologies and vary widely between sources. Treat them as orders of magnitude rather than precise counts.
- Monthly cost figures cited for couples (Thailand ~$2,000, Ecuador $1,600 to $2,200, Costa Rica $2,800 to $3,500, Vietnam ~$1,800) reflect commonly reported expat budgets and were not independently sourced here.
