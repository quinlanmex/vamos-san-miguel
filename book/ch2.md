# Chapter 2: Federal Income Taxes & the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion

The first time we filed taxes from abroad, we did it at a kitchen table in Costa Rica that shook every time one of our kids walked past. The air outside was thick and humming with insects and the occasional call of a howler monkey, the kind of night where everything feels loud except you. My wife sat across from me in the dim light, kids asleep, laptop once again open between us like a window into either freedom or disaster.

We had just finished entering our return using the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE), an obscure section of the U.S. tax code no one had ever mentioned to us back home. I kept refreshing the screen, waiting for a number to jump out and slap me. It didn't. Instead, the tax software kept showing the same impossible number:

$0 federal income tax.

Not a deduction.

Not a credit.

An exclusion. As if the income never existed.

"This can't be right," I said.

My wife leaned in. "It's what the accountant said."

She shrugged the way someone does when they know they should be happy but aren't sure they are allowed to be.

That night, instead of celebrating, I fell down an anxious research tunnel. I reread IRS Publication 54 multiple times. I dug through tax forums and abandoned blogs, the kind that looked like they hadn't been touched in a decade, searching for someone who had asked my exact question and lived to tell about it. I emailed two accountants with the same paranoid question phrased three different ways.

By morning, one of them replied: "You qualify. This is normal. This is what FEIE is designed for."

Normal. The word felt insane. But it was true. And once I understood why it was true, the full financial implications of living abroad snapped into place.

## Why FEIE Feels "Too Good to Be True"

Most people encounter FEIE with immediate suspicion. We grow up believing the IRS is an omnipresent guardian of morality. So when a legal mechanism reduces federal income tax to as low as zero, it triggers a feeling that something must be wrong.

That instinct misreads what is happening. You haven't found a loophole. You have found how the law works, and it only works this way if your situation fits certain requirements.

### The Moral Discomfort

People routinely say things like: "I don't want to abuse the system," or "I'd rather just pay my fair share."

What matters is the structure of what you are actually doing. The FEIE doesn't eliminate your tax burden entirely. You pay Social Security. You pay Medicare. You pay state taxes if you maintain residency in states with income taxes (more on that in the next Chapter). You pay value-added taxes on everything you buy.

What you don't pay is federal income tax on salary earned abroad up to certain limits, a policy designed to reduce double taxation when Americans live and work overseas. The tax code repeatedly reflects this principle: the U.S. tries to avoid taxing the same labor income twice when Americans are genuinely based abroad.

Meanwhile, large corporations take the same approach of evaluating the tax code and acting according to its rules and allowances:

- License intellectual property across jurisdictions
- Shift income through international subsidiaries
- Optimize cost centers
- Use cross-border structures to manage tax exposure

All day. Every day. For decades. None of that is improvisation. None of it is hidden. It is the tax code functioning as written. The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion is the individual version: explicit, disclosed, and written into law.

## What is the FEIE?

The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE) allows you to exclude up to:

- The annual FEIE limit per qualifying person (up to $130,000 in 2025) [1]
- Double that limit for a married couple if both qualify (up to $260,000 in 2025) [1]

This amount increases annually with inflation. If you perform your work while physically outside the United States, and qualify under one of two FEIE tests, your income is treated as if it never existed for federal income tax purposes.

Before getting into the two tests, let's review a few important caveats.

### Earned Income Only

FEIE applies only to income you earn through work. It does not apply to dividends, distributions, interest, capital gains, rental income, or royalties. Only labor income counts.

### Client Location Doesn't Matter

Even if your clients are entirely in the U.S., your income qualifies as foreign-earned because you performed the work abroad. Your location determines tax treatment. Client location does not.

### Passive Income Remains Fully Taxable

Everything not tied to your labor stays fully taxable under standard rules. FEIE accomplishes one thing, and does it extremely well: reducing your federal tax liability on income earned while living abroad.

A quick caveat: in some scenarios, especially with high unearned income or certain business structures, the Foreign Tax Credit can outperform FEIE. Your CPA should model both. This book focuses on FEIE because it works for the majority of remote workers and small-business owners, but it is not universally optimal.

## How to Qualify for FEIE

Two paths exist to FEIE qualification. One is mathematical; the other is rooted in where your life is actually built.

### Path 1: Physical Presence Test (PPT)

Requirement: 330 full days outside the U.S. in any 12-month period. [1]

This doesn't have to match the calendar year. You can use any rolling 12-month window. A full day means 24 consecutive hours outside U.S. borders.

You also don't need 330 consecutive days. You need 330 days total within your 12-month window. Missing a day here or there doesn't disqualify you if you remain above 330 overall. If you are physically on U.S. soil at any point during a calendar day (airport included), that day won't count as a full day abroad. If you are cutting it close, that detail matters.

If you leave after April 15 and won't reach 330 days before the tax filing deadline, you can choose to file Form 4868 to extend your tax filing deadline to October 15.

To document your number of days abroad during any 12-month period, you can use:

- Passport stamps
- Credit card statements showing foreign activity
- Flight itineraries
- Airbnb receipts or other housing documentation
- A simple travel spreadsheet

Digital-only documentation is usually adequate if it is consistent and complete. Track days, keep receipts, and make your documentation consistent with your claimed days abroad.

**PPT Example 1: Leaving the U.S. on March 15.** If you leave the U.S. on March 15, 2025, you might use the period March 16, 2025 to March 15, 2026. If you accumulate 330 full days abroad during that window, you qualify for the portion of the year you were abroad.

**PPT Example 2: Frequent Traveler.** Portugal to Thailand to Mexico to Japan to Bali. For PPT day-counting purposes, days outside the U.S. count regardless of which country you are in.

**PPT Example 3: When PPT Becomes Difficult.** If you return to the U.S. for 45 days of holidays, 12 days for a wedding, 18 days for doctors, and 15 days for business, that is 90 days in the U.S., leaving only 275 abroad. Not sufficient. Solutions: consider switching to BFR, adjusting travel patterns, or shifting your 12-month window.

**PPT Example 4: Using the Extension Mechanism.** Move abroad in June. By April 15 of the next year, you won't have 330 days yet. Consider filing Form 4868, extending your tax filing deadline to October 15. Now you qualify.

### Path 2: Bona Fide Residency (BFR)

BFR requirement: Reside in a foreign country for an entire calendar year (January 1 through December 31), and actually establish a life in a single location.

In plain English: your tax home has to be abroad, and your day-to-day life has to look like it is actually based there, where you are regularly living and working. Facts and circumstances, not paperwork.

If you arrive in Mexico mid-year, you can't use BFR until the next full calendar year. For this reason, many families start under PPT, then transition to BFR once settled.

To qualify under BFR, the IRS needs evidence that you genuinely live where you claim to live, such as one or more of the following:

- Residency card
- Long-term lease or home ownership
- Community involvement
- School enrollment for children

Once you qualify under BFR, in practice many long-term residents spend several months per year in the U.S. without jeopardizing FEIE status. However, bona fide residency determinations remain facts-and-circumstances based, and extended U.S. presence can increase scrutiny depending on the specifics of your individual situation.

In future years after your initial qualifying year, you don't need to be in-country on January 1 or December 31. That timing requirement applies only to the first qualification year.

**BFR Example 1: The Mexico Settler.** Move to Mexico City in December 2025. You are physically present on January 1, 2026. Sign a lease, obtain residency cards, enroll children in school, build a local life. You remain in Mexico on December 31, 2026. You qualify for FEIE under BFR for all of 2026. Even if you travel extensively throughout the year and spend only 250 days outside the U.S., you still qualify because Mexico is your clear, established home for the full calendar year.

**BFR Example 2: When BFR Doesn't Develop.** If you transition between countries, lack a residency permit, or your tax home still appears to be in the U.S., BFR collapses. PPT becomes the safer approach. Again, many expats start under PPT, then transition to BFR if they establish roots. Some stay on PPT to keep things simpler.

## FBAR and FATCA: Two Compliance Forms You Cannot Miss

Before going further, two filing obligations deserve their own treatment. They are not part of FEIE, but they apply to nearly every American living abroad, and missing them carries penalties that dwarf the income tax savings FEIE provides.

### FBAR

If the total value of all your foreign bank accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point last year, even for a single day, you are required to file a Foreign Bank Account Report (FBAR). [3]

"Foreign accounts" is broader than you might think. It includes:

- Checking and savings accounts
- Brokerage and investment accounts
- Accounts you have signatory authority over, even if the money isn't yours (a joint account with a spouse, for example, or an employer account you manage)

The FBAR is filed electronically to FinCEN, separately from your tax return, by April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15.

The penalty for missing it: [3]

- Non-willful violation: up to $10,000 per account, per year
- Willful violation: $100,000 or 50 percent of the account balance, whichever is greater
- Criminal penalties are possible for intentional non-filing

### FATCA

Separate from FBAR, FATCA requires you to file Form 8938 with your tax return if your foreign financial assets exceed: [4]

- $200,000 (single) or $400,000 (married filing jointly) at year-end, for taxpayers living abroad

FATCA covers foreign bank accounts, investment accounts, ownership in foreign entities, and foreign pensions. The penalties for missing it start at $10,000, with up to $50,000 for continued non-filing after IRS notice. [4]

One important note: FBAR and FATCA overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Many Americans abroad must file both. Filing one does not satisfy the other.

## The Housing Exclusion for Additional Savings

If FEIE didn't exist, the housing exclusion alone would be a serious tax benefit for expats. The housing exclusion allows you to exclude qualified housing expenses above a base amount, up to a location-specific limit.

Formula: Qualified Housing Costs minus Published Base Amount equals Excludable Amount, subject to the local cap.

### Base Amount (2025)

Base amount equals 16 percent of the FEIE. For 2025: $20,800 per qualifying person. [2]

### Mexico City Example (2025)

Mexico City Base Amount: $20,800 in 2025.

Rent: $24,000. Plus utilities: $3,600. Total housing: $27,600.

Mexico City's local cap is $47,900, which is higher than your $27,600 in qualified expenses, so the full $27,600 enters the formula. [2] Subtract the base amount: $27,600 minus $20,800 equals $6,800. That $6,800 is what gets excluded under the housing exclusion. The remainder is paid with after-tax dollars as normal.

If both spouses qualify, each can claim the exclusion separately. The specific numbers change annually; the structure of the calculation does not. This is how many expat families exclude another $10,000 to $30,000 of taxable income on top of FEIE.

### Location-Specific Limits (2025)

Most Mexican cities have a local cap of approximately $39,000 that changes annually. [2] Higher-cost cities have higher caps. For example, Mexico City's cap was $47,900 in 2025. Cities with higher assigned caps include Ciudad Juárez, Cuernavaca, Guadalajara, Hermosillo, Matamoros, Mazatlán, Mérida, Mexico City, Monterrey, Nogales, Nuevo Laredo, Tijuana, and Veracruz. [2] The IRS publishes these caps each year; your accountant can pull the current table annually.

## Five Common FEIE Mistakes

Most of the surprises in FEIE strategy don't come from the exclusion itself. They come from ignoring what it doesn't cover:

- **Residency taxes and structure matter.** FEIE eliminates federal tax only. If you maintain state residency (California, New York, etc.), you still owe state tax. More on this in the next chapter. Self-employment tax runs about 15.3 percent on net earnings, and FEIE doesn't touch it. [5]
- **Some government programs ignore FEIE.** Your excluded salary still counts for health insurance subsidies (ACA) and certain surtaxes (NIIT). This can turn "tax savings" into surprise clawbacks if not modeled correctly.
- **Travel days and U.S. days need careful attention.** One miscounted day can drop you below 330. If you are cutting it close, that can wreck the year. A trip home for the holidays, a delayed flight stuck in a U.S. airport, a quick two-day U.S. doctor's visit: any of these get subtracted from your count.
- **Unearned income stays fully taxable.** Rental income, investment income, distributions from businesses: FEIE doesn't touch these. Your overall tax picture depends on what else you are earning.
- **"Bona fide" is facts-and-circumstances.** You can't paperwork your way into BFR. The IRS looks at the totality of your life abroad, not just your residency card.

## Advanced Module: Business Owners, Freelancers & S-Corp Structures

Skip this section if you are a W-2 employee.

Business owners have additional levers, but also additional traps.

If you are a freelancer, contractor, consultant, or business owner, you have a distinctive advantage: you can control how your income is classified for U.S. tax purposes. Employees cannot do this. Their wages are wages.

But you also have additional traps. At a high level:

- Salary is earned income, eligible for FEIE
- Distributions are not earned income, fully taxable
- Payroll tax applies only to salary

FEIE eliminates income tax on salary, not payroll tax, and not taxes on distributions.

Here are some tradeoffs you need to navigate as a business owner or freelancer:

- Lower salary: lower payroll tax, lower amount eligible for FEIE, higher taxable distributions, less retirement contribution room
- Higher salary: higher payroll tax, higher amount eligible for FEIE, fewer taxable distributions, more retirement contribution room

The IRS also expects reasonable compensation. Extremely low salaries increase scrutiny.

### S-Corp Owners Only: The Salary/Distribution Tradeoff

For many business owners and freelancers, S-Corp savings tend to emerge once annual profit reaches roughly $80,000 to $100,000. Below that, or in more complex situations, the added payroll and administration often outweigh the benefit. Because breakpoints vary by facts, CPAs typically model both structures before recommending the best path forward.

### The Three S-Corp Salary Scenarios (2025)

Assumptions for all three scenarios:

- Business profit (before salary): $160,000
- Married filing jointly (MFJ)
- Full FEIE available
- Salary excluded under FEIE
- Distributions are taxable
- Payroll tax (FICA): 15.3 percent of salary [5]
- Standard deduction (2025): $30,000 [7]
- S-Corp wage structure
- No state income tax (see next chapter for more details)

Important stacking rule: although FEIE excludes salary from income tax, excluded wages still determine the marginal tax bracket that is applied to the rest of your taxable income (such as distributions). This effect is incorporated in the examples below.

#### Scenario A: Low Salary, High Distribution

Total income: $160,000. Salary: $40,000 (excluded under FEIE). Distribution: $120,000.

Income tax: $120,000 distribution minus $30,000 standard deduction equals $90,000 taxable. Approximate federal tax: $11,045.

Payroll tax (FICA): $40,000 salary times 15.3 percent equals $6,120.

Total federal tax: $17,165.

Why this is dangerous: a $40,000 salary on $160,000 of profit is a 25 percent ratio. This is precisely the structure the IRS targets for "unreasonably low salary" audits. That's risky.

#### Scenario B: Middle Salary, Middle Distribution

Total income: $160,000. Salary: $70,000 (excluded under FEIE). Distribution: $90,000.

Income tax: $90,000 distribution minus $30,000 standard deduction equals $60,000 taxable. Approximate federal tax: $4,745.

Payroll tax (FICA): $70,000 salary times 15.3 percent equals $10,710.

Total federal tax: $15,455.

Why this often works well: salary is excluded under FEIE. Distribution is smaller, so federal tax is lower. Salary is highly defensible as reasonable compensation. In practice, this range frequently balances tax efficiency and audit defensibility for many expat owners.

#### Scenario C: High Salary, Low Distribution

Total income: $160,000. Salary: $100,000 (excluded under FEIE). Distribution: $60,000.

Income tax: $60,000 distribution minus $30,000 standard deduction equals $30,000 taxable. Approximate federal tax: $3,145.

Payroll tax (FICA): $100,000 salary times 15.3 percent equals $15,300.

Total federal tax: $18,445.

Potential downside: at higher income tiers, distributions can cross the Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) threshold for the Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT), an additional 3.8 percent surtax on investment income. [6] This is one structural reason the mid-range scenario often positions owners better.

### Unified Comparison Table

| Salary Scenario | Salary | Payroll Tax | Income Tax | Total Tax | IRS Defensibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A (Low) | $40k | $6,120 | $11,045 | $17,165 | Weak |
| B (Mid) | $70k | $10,710 | $4,745 | $15,455 | Strong |
| C (High) | $100k | $15,300 | $3,145 | $18,445 | Very Strong |

Defensibility reflects how clearly the salary aligns with reasonable compensation for work performed.

### The Pattern: Mid-Range Salary

Across the scenarios, a mid-range salary often balances tax efficiency, retirement contributions, and audit defensibility better than very low or very high salary approaches. It can produce the lowest total tax in these three scenarios and may offer a more defensible compensation posture than very low salary.

### How CPAs Approach Reasonable Salary Defensibility

Many successful expat filings we have reviewed show CPAs consistently applying the following framework:

Reasonable expat S-Corp salary = U.S. market salary for your role multiplied by 50 to 70 percent.

It is not an IRS mandate. It is a pattern that appears defensible in practice. Going too low invites scrutiny; the IRS expects reasonable compensation for work performed. Going too high triggers unnecessary FICA. The 50 to 70 percent band represents where most CPAs sanity-check salary ranges to balance defensibility against efficiency.

Your CPA should model the actual numbers for your specific situation.

## Implementation Roadmap

The following reflects sequencing patterns many expats follow; individual circumstances vary substantially based on residency status, visa timing, business structure, and state tax obligations.

### Phase 1: Before Leaving the U.S.

For everyone:

- Choose your FEIE qualification path (PPT vs BFR)
- Identify your planned departure date
- Map out your 330-day window or first Bona Fide Residency year
- Set up a FEIE documentation system

If you are employed (W-2): you don't need a business structure. Your employer will continue normal payroll; FEIE applies automatically when you file taxes.

If you are self-employed or run a business:

- Evaluate whether an S-Corp election makes sense (commonly above $80,000 annual profit)
- If using S-Corp, plan your salary range (the 50 to 70 percent heuristic applies to many situations)
- Plan distributions vs salary for the year and set up payroll

### Phase 2: After Arrival Abroad

For everyone:

- Begin tracking days abroad
- Document residency: lease, local activity
- Keep passport stamps, statements, travel log
- Start quarterly documentation reviews

### Phase 3: Running Your Income Structure Abroad

For W-2 employees: nothing structural to manage. FEIE applies to wages on Form 2555. Documentation is your primary task.

For freelancers, contractors, and business owners:

- Run payroll if using S-Corp
- Make employee plus employer retirement contributions
- Track distributions
- Keep accounting books aligned with your salary structure

### Phase 4: Annual Requirements (Everyone)

- File Form 2555 (FEIE)
- File U.S. tax return normally
- File FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) if foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point
- File Form 8938 (FATCA) if foreign assets exceed thresholds
- If using S-Corp: file 1120-S, issue K-1
- Evaluate FEIE vs BFR for the following year
- Reassess salary (if self-employed) and retirement contributions
