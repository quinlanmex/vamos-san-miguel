# Chapter 12: Housing

## The balcony

Our Puerto Vallarta condo had a view that stopped conversations. It also had a twenty-foot drop-off on one side of the balcony, the kind of gap that a photograph flattens into nothing and a toddler treats as an invitation. We noticed it on day two, after we had signed.

So we hired a local gardener to line the edge with enormous planters, filled them with beautiful plants, and built ourselves a barrier that looked like a design choice instead of a panic response. It worked. It also cost money and time we had not budgeted, and it made leaving that condo harder than it should have been, because by then we had turned someone else's hazard into our home.

That is the whole chapter in one story. The house you pick is the multiplier on everything else you build in Mexico. Get it right and your daily life runs quietly in the background. Get it wrong and you spend your energy managing the house instead of living in it. The good news is that the mistakes are learnable in advance, which is cheaper than learning them the way we did.

By now you have done Stage 1: a short scouting stay in a furnished rental while you walked the neighborhoods and figured out where you actually want to be. This chapter is Stage 2, the long-term lease, where the real work and the real savings begin. Before you dive into the mechanics, understand that this decision sits inside a larger fork you will face in most expat towns: whether to stabilize inside a gated *fraccionamiento* (a residential development with controlled security and managed infrastructure) or inside a traditional neighborhood. That choice shapes what your first year feels like and which doors open after it. We will get there. First, the plumbing of how renting works here.

## How Mexican rentals actually work

The best long-term rentals in Mexico do not appear on Zillow or Apartments.com. They live in the heads and phones of local realtors who maintain relationships with owners and often hold quiet access to the most desirable properties. If you search only the sites you already know, you are seeing the leftovers.

Contact at least two realtors. They tend to represent different portfolios, so two agents will not show you the same inventory. Your instinct will be to pick the one you click with personally. Do that, but weight one factor above likability: how connected they are. A well-connected realtor can call around their network and surface listings that never hit the market at all. Someone who has been working the same town for years and knows everyone is worth more to you than someone charming who started last spring.

During that first phone or WhatsApp conversation, be specific about what you need. Vagueness wastes everyone's week. Cover:

- **Property type.** Condo, apartment, single-family home, or studio. Each carries different maintenance duties and community dynamics.
- **Monthly budget.** Include utilities in the number if you can. Mexican utility costs swing hard with usage and season, and a cheap-looking rent can hide an expensive summer.
- **Furnishing level.** Fully furnished, partially furnished, or unfurnished. Mexican "furnished" ranges from "complete down to the silverware" to "there is a bed and a table."
- **Kitchen reality.** Full appliances? How complete are the cookware and dishes? Can you actually cook a meal, or only reheat one?
- **Linens and basics.** Sheets, towels, cleaning supplies. These details matter enormously when you arrive with suitcases instead of a moving truck.
- **Neighborhood.** Walkable, quiet, central, expat-friendly. Define each word, because your "quiet" and the realtor's "quiet" may not match.
- **Space.** Bedrooms, bathrooms, a workspace for remote work, outdoor space, a layout that survives children.
- **Climate control.** Which rooms have air conditioning, and what happens when the power drops during a heat wave.
- **Family needs.** Kid-friendly building and streets, playground access, school proximity.

### The Mexico-specific wrinkle nobody warns you about

Ask about domestic help and maintenance, because in Mexico it is often baked into the rental in ways that change your real monthly cost.

Garden and major exterior maintenance are almost always the landlord's responsibility, handled by their staff or a service they pay directly [1]. Housekeeping is where it gets variable. In some rentals the landlord already has a trusted housekeeper who comes on fixed days, and you pay their hourly rate directly, often in the range of $3 to $5 USD (roughly 50 to 90 pesos) per hour at the time of writing [1]. In others the landlord keeps paying for housekeeping as part of the package and simply expects you to coordinate access and supplies.

So ask three plain questions:

1. "Who pays for gardening and exterior maintenance, me or the owner?"
2. "Is there an existing housekeeper? How often do they come, and who pays them?"
3. "If we would rather not have regular housekeeping, can we opt out, or is it part of the deal?"

Those answers give you a truer picture of your monthly cost and your weekly time savings. There is a full chapter on domestic help later. Here your only job is to learn what is already attached to the house, and whether that attachment is a feature or a bug for your family.

## Seeing the place in person

Once a realtor has inventory that fits, go look. In person. This is not optional. You are committing to a year, and photos lie with a straight face about space, light, noise, and whether the walls are structurally serious.

Visit under different conditions. A calm Tuesday afternoon viewing will not reveal the weekend party house next door or the rooster who runs the 5 a.m. shift. See every serious contender in daylight and after dark, on a weekday and a weekend. If you have kids, come by when children would normally be outside.

Walk the neighborhood with your actual life in mind. Walk to the grocery store, the pharmacy, the restaurants you would use. If you have children, walk to the parks and the school. Notice sidewalks, lighting, and how safe it feels at different hours. If a simple grocery run leaves you winded, picture doing it with a week of supplies and two tired kids.

Then test the details that decide daily comfort. Turn on every faucet and check the water pressure. Run the internet speed test from more than one room. Run the air conditioning and ask what a month of it costs. Check cell signal throughout the property. Stand by the windows and listen for traffic, construction, and party-prone neighbors during the hours you would work or sleep.

Ask about the seasons, because the property changes with them. Rainy-season flooding, summer heat, winter cold snaps, dry-season construction. That peaceful winter rental can become a furnace in July or a job site in April.

If a landlord will allow it, negotiate a one-week trial before you sign the full lease. A week of actual living surfaces what a viewing never will: noise patterns, water reliability, internet stability, and whether the local bat colony considers your terrace contested territory. Not every landlord will say yes. It costs nothing to ask.

## Negotiating the lease

Mexican rental agreements run on different assumptions than American ones. Knowing the differences ahead of time turns surprises into ordinary negotiation.

### Language and what is legally binding

Get the lease in both English and Spanish. The Spanish version is the one that binds you, because a contract needs to be in Spanish to be enforceable here, and any dispute will turn on that text [2]. You still need to understand exactly what you are signing, so run both versions through an AI tool like ChatGPT to compare them line by line and confirm they say the same thing. If they diverge on anything that matters, stop and get it fixed before you sign.

### Deposit and payment

Standard practice is one month's deposit plus first month's rent, paid upfront. Negotiate the deposit return timeline while you are at it. Landlords often propose 60 days to return your deposit. Fifteen days after move-out is more reasonable and protects you from a cash-flow gap.

Put the payment method in the contract. Wire transfers through a service like Wise create documentation and are generally reliable. Avoid cash, which leaves no paper trail for either side.

### The fiador

In some markets and at higher price points, owners ask for a *fiador*, a local co-signer who guarantees the lease and typically must own debt-free property in the same Mexican state, so the landlord can place a lien on it if things go wrong [3]. Most new expats do not have one. If you are asked for a fiador you cannot provide, you can often negotiate around it with a larger deposit, several months paid up front, or a letter of reference from your employer or bank. We have never been asked for one, but expats we have talked to have run into it, especially for high-end properties in areas with a thinner expat presence.

### Exchange-rate risk

Most long-term leases in Mexico quote rent in pesos, and you pay in pesos. That is usually what you want, since your daily spending is in pesos too. There is a hidden variable Americans tend to miss, though: the exchange rate.

Say you find a comfortable three-bedroom cabin for 35,000 pesos per month. At 25 pesos to the dollar, that is about $1,400 USD. If the peso strengthens to 17 per dollar, that same 35,000 pesos now costs you $2,060 USD. The house did not change. Your effective rent did, by nearly $700 a month.

You have a few ways to handle it, depending on your income and appetite for risk:

- Stay in pesos and accept the swings as part of the deal.
- Negotiate a USD anchor, agreeing that rent adjusts annually within a defined band so neither side takes a beating.
- In rare cases, quote the rent in USD directly, with a clear written understanding of how and when conversion happens at payment.

There is a whole chapter on money and payments where we look at your broader currency strategy. In the housing context, your only job is to be intentional. Do not stumble into a lease that feels affordable at today's rate and turns painful when the peso moves against you.

### Duration and flexibility

Most leases convert to month-to-month after the first year automatically. Decide whether you want that flexibility or would rather renegotiate terms each year on purpose.

Do not be shy about the monthly rent. Mexican landlords are often far more flexible on price than their American counterparts. Submit a lower offer, especially if your realtor can point to comparable properties at better rates. The worst answer is no, and you will be surprised how often it is not no.

Ask for early-termination options too. A one-month penalty for breaking the lease early buys you real flexibility if your circumstances change. Not every landlord agrees, but it is worth requesting, particularly if you are still recovering from a previous housing disaster.

### Landlord obligations, in writing

Add language about response times for repairs. "Within one business day" for urgent problems like plumbing or electrical keeps a small failure from becoming a long inconvenience.

Include a clause that lets you terminate if the landlord fails to meet their obligations. This gives you recourse when promised amenities or services do not materialize.

A close friend of ours in Valle de Bravo learned this the expensive way. They got a great deal on a house that was nearly finished, with the landlord promising the work would wrap before move-in. Four months into the lease the work still was not done, and they had to fight to be released. Had the promise to complete the work been written into the lease, there would have been little to argue about.

### Move-in and move-out

Document the property's condition thoroughly on the way in. Shoot detailed video of every room, with close-ups of any existing damage, and send it to both the landlord and the realtor over WhatsApp so there is a timestamped record.

Do the same on the way out, and leave the place cleaner than you found it. That small investment in goodwill pays off in your deposit return and in the references you will want for your next home.

## What the process actually looks like

In practice, signing a lease in Mexico tends to follow the same sequence:

1. A WhatsApp inquiry to a realtor or directly to the owner about a specific property.
2. An in-person tour of one or more homes, often scheduled back to back.
3. A verbal agreement on the basics: rent, start date, lease length, what is included.
4. A draft lease in Spanish, prepared by the owner's attorney or realtor.
5. Translation and review, whether by a bilingual friend or realtor, a paid translator, or an AI tool comparing the two versions line by line.
6. Negotiation of the key terms: deposit, pets, early termination, included services.
7. Signing, usually in person, occasionally by scanned PDF.
8. Payment of deposit and first month's rent, typically by bank transfer.
9. A move-in walkthrough with photos, video, and an inventory of furniture and appliances.
10. Handover of keys, codes, and contacts: property manager, gardener, housekeeper, building admin.

Different cities and price points add their own wrinkles. You do not need to memorize every variant. You need the general shape, so that nothing anyone does feels like a surprise negotiating tactic.

## The fraccionamiento question

Once you are seriously comparing neighborhoods, you will run into *fraccionamientos*, the gated communities with controlled access, security staff, and a self-contained world of amenities. In expat-heavy towns like Puerto Vallarta, San Miguel de Allende, Mérida, and the suburbs of Mexico City, they dominate the housing landscape for Americans who want an extra layer of structure.

The appeal is real and immediate. Controlled entry, security around the clock, maintained landscaping, often a pool or a gym, and instant access to a ready-made community of other expats going through the same transition. You move in and you are not alone. There are already people to ask about doctors, restaurants, and which streets to skip. Some upscale developments even run private transport to downtown shopping and medical appointments, so you never fight for parking.

The amenity list leaves out the lifestyle that comes attached, and you only see it clearly once you have lived inside one. Most fraccionamientos sit on the outskirts of town, because land is cheaper where you lack the density and location premium of an established neighborhood. That location creates a quiet friction: the walkable, historic, market-rich parts of Mexico are a car ride away. You do not wander into a café because you happened to be walking past. You do not become a regular anywhere. You live in a parallel ecosystem optimized for comfort and sealed off from the wider community.

The difference from a traditional neighborhood goes deeper than aesthetics. It changes how fast you integrate. In a traditional neighborhood, friction forces familiarity. You negotiate with the water-delivery guy, you learn the name of the owner at the corner store, you work through complications alongside local friends who have done it a hundred times. In a fraccionamiento those friction points vanish, and so does the process that turns you from a visitor into a resident.

**A fraccionamiento serves you well when:**

- **You are establishing stability with young children.** Kids run on predictability, and gated communities deliver it: consistent rules, managed spaces, other families in the same transition. The cost, that your kids spend less time inside authentic Mexican daily life, matters less during the settling-in phase than it would in years three through five.
- **Your Spanish is limited and you want to reduce friction while you learn.** These communities cluster English speakers and often employ bilingual staff, which buys you a learning runway without the daily pressure of doing everything in a language you are still acquiring.
- **You are staying a year or less and want turnkey infrastructure.** If you are not building long-term roots, the isolation trade-off barely applies. You get operational certainty without worrying about the depth of your integration.
- **You value operational certainty over cultural immersion.** Some people simply prefer reliable utilities, predictable maintenance, and managed standards, often at higher quality than comparable traditional-neighborhood options. That is a legitimate preference, not a moral failing.
- **You want people in your exact situation during a vulnerable stretch.** The value of "I am not the only one figuring this out" is easy to underestimate in your arrival phase, and that bridge to other expats speeds up your stabilization.

**A fraccionamiento costs you when:**

- **You want community beyond the expat bubble.** These places can become echo chambers where your friendships stay mostly American. Deep integration means working through complexity with locals who know the systems cold.
- **You value walkability and proximity to daily culture.** If casual commerce and spontaneous cultural exposure matter to you, a gated community on the edge of town constrains all of it.
- **You are planning to stay long-term.** After year two or three, the gates can start to feel confining once you have the confidence and language to operate on your own. Many expats describe exactly this moment, when the security that once felt protective begins to feel like a fence.
- **You prefer running your own life.** HOA rules, established social structures, and pre-designed systems can feel infantilizing if you value autonomy.

The honest pattern: many successful long-term families start in a fraccionamiento for security and psychological comfort, then move to a traditional neighborhood once they have the confidence and local knowledge to operate independently. Some stay because the low maintenance and operational certainty outweigh the cultural trade-off. Others, us included, skip fraccionamientos entirely and take the friction from day one. Think of a fraccionamiento as a housing on-ramp, stable and forgiving, until you are ready to drive without training wheels. It can also be a genuinely good long-term home when the lifestyle match is real.

If you are considering one, ask these during your evaluation:

- "What is the actual walk time to downtown and neighborhood commerce?" Measured on foot from the main gate, not from the marketing brochure.
- "How often do power outages happen here, and does the community have a backup generator?"
- "How many expats live here, and how tight is that social network?" A feature for some, a constraint for others.
- "Can I rent month-to-month, or does the community require longer leases?" Some enforce their own lease minimums beyond what the individual landlord would allow.
- "Are there real sidewalks and pedestrian infrastructure, or is everything built around cars?"
- "What does the HOA restrict, from pets and guests to noise hours, renovations, and short-term rentals?" These rules are often stricter than in traditional neighborhoods and deserve clarity before you commit.
- "What is the turnover rate among expats here?" High turnover suggests people leave once they understand the limits, or once they graduate out.

## Furnished versus unfurnished

You will meet two rental models, each with its own advantages and its own hidden costs.

| | Furnished | Unfurnished |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Arrival** | Easy. Land with suitcases and start living. | Slow. You furnish and equip a house first. |
| **Rent** | Higher. | Usually much lower. |
| **Upfront cost** | Minimal. | Significant. You buy furniture, kitchen, electronics. |
| **Control** | Low. You inherit someone else's taste, mattress, and appliance reliability. | High. You build the comfort you actually want. |
| **When the AC fails** | You depend on the landlord's speed and standards. | You own the fix and the timeline. |
| **On move-out** | Walk away. | You are responsible for moving your things. |

We rented fully furnished until we finally bought our own home. That kept us nimble for three years while we found the right spot.

There is no universally correct answer. Your choice depends on your time horizon (one year or five?), your cash position, and your tolerance for living inside someone else's comfort standards. The only real mistake is defaulting into one by accident instead of choosing it on purpose.

## The infrastructure reality check

Even well-kept Mexican properties may not match American infrastructure standards. Treat that as part of the adjustment rather than a defect.

**Water.** Systems here may use rooftop tanks, pressure pumps, or gravity feeds that behave differently from a direct municipal connection. Learn your specific setup and its maintenance needs. Confirm that all tanks have been cleaned recently, or will be before you move in, and check their cleanliness with your own eyes.

**Electrical.** The outlets and voltage match the United States, so your devices plug in and work: Mexico runs on 127V at 60Hz with Type A and Type B plugs, effectively the same as the US standard [4]. Power outages, though, are more common than you are used to. If your home has no generator, invest in battery backup for your essential devices. We covered power-backup strategy in detail in the Working Remotely chapter. Plan for it from the beginning, especially in smaller towns.

**Internet.** Speed varies dramatically by neighborhood and provider. Test actual speeds during peak hours, not the number printed on the brochure.

**Security.** Homes range from basic locks to full alarm systems. Understand what is provided and what you may want to add.

**Climate control.** This is the line between comfort and misery. Know which rooms have cooling, what the electricity will cost, and what the backup plan is when the power drops.

## Insurance: your stuff versus your liability

A quiet housing trap for Americans is assuming insurance works the way it does back home. It does not, and that is good news once you understand the split.

If you are renting fully furnished and arriving with little more than clothes, laptops, and a few personal items, you may not need a traditional renters policy at all. Most of the physical contents belong to the landlord, and their homeowners policy is what protects that furniture and those appliances.

Your real exposure is liability. If a guest trips on your stairs, or your kid's science experiment cracks a neighbor's window, you want protection that follows you rather than the building. In Mexico that usually means a personal liability policy, often available as a standalone product or bundled with other coverage.

If you are renting unfurnished or semi-furnished and buying your own furniture, kitchen, and electronics, a renters-style policy for your contents matters more, because you are building a household balance sheet in Mexico that deserves protection.

| Your situation | What to focus on |
| --- | --- |
| Fully furnished, light personal items | Personal liability coverage |
| Unfurnished, you buying most contents | Renters-style contents coverage, plus liability |

Do not DIY this through random online forms. Find a local insurance agent who regularly works with foreigners (ask your realtor for a referral) and ask three specific questions:

1. What covers my liability if someone is injured in or because of my home?
2. What covers the contents I own and am bringing into the property?
3. How do claims actually work in practice, and who do I call, in what order?

The goal is not to insure every fork and throw pillow. It is to keep one unlucky accident from turning your Mexican housing experiment into an expensive legal lesson.

## The address wildcard

One more infrastructure surprise, especially outside the major cities: addresses often barely exist.

In some towns your official address reads like a set of pirate directions. "300 meters past the Oxxo, next to Rancho Borlena, blue gate." Delivery drivers will call you from the highway. Friends will WhatsApp for a dropped pin.

This is not a glitch in the Matrix. It is how many Mexican municipalities grew up. For you it means two things. Expect to lean heavily on WhatsApp location sharing and map pins. And when you are evaluating a property, ask the landlord or the neighbors how delivery services and ride-shares actually handle the location in practice. We go deeper on delivery logistics in the Money and Payments and Shopping and Delivery chapters.

## Building the long-term relationship

Your relationship with your landlord and property manager runs well past the lease signing. In Mexican culture, personal relationships shape business outcomes more than they typically do in an American transaction.

Keep communication regular and respectful. Report issues promptly and clearly. Pay rent on time, every time. Treat the property with obvious care. These investments pay off precisely when you need flexibility, a fast repair, or a positive reference for your next place, and they build the broader expat reputation that makes the process smoother for the Americans who follow you.

## The bottom line

Your housing foundation is the multiplier on everything else you build in Mexico. Ours took longer and cost more than we expected, and it handed us a priceless filter in return. Now, when we walk into a home, we know almost immediately whether it is a match. The bats, the heat, the stunning and unlivable views: those were tuition, and the return on that tuition has paid out ever since.

Invest the time to get this right, and every other system in your Mexican life falls into place more easily.

### Your next action

Before you start house hunting, do these three things:

1. **Write down your non-negotiables and your preferences, in two separate columns.** Be honest about which requirements are absolute and which you could bend. Factor in seasonal weather, family needs, noise tolerance, walkability, and whether fraccionamiento stability matters more to you than traditional-neighborhood integration. Learn from our balcony: a beautiful view does not offset a daily hazard or a daily inconvenience.
2. **Line up 3 to 4 neighborhoods and two well-connected realtors.** Confirm your WhatsApp works internationally, get your Spanish translation app ready, and gather the documents a lease application may want: proof of income, references, and identification.
3. **Book one month in an Airbnb in your target destination, positioned for easy exploration of several neighborhoods.** Use it as your Stage 1 base while you walk the streets at different hours. And make sure it has all its walls.
