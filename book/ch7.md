# Chapter 7: Working Remotely

## The camera angle I used to fuss over

For years I framed my shots. This was before background filters, so I would tilt the laptop, angle the camera against a blank wall, choose the one corner of wherever I was that could pass for a normal office. When a client asked where I was calling from, I gave a vague answer and changed the subject. I treated my location like a secret that could cost me the deal.

That was stupid.

Last month I dialed into a call from Oaxaca, in the middle of a two-week worldschooling hub with my kids, and a major client heard a brief connection hiccup. I mentioned where I was, casually, by way of apology. His response: "Wow, that's impressive. We should talk about how you're pulling that off." The whole conversation shifted. I stopped being another vendor and became someone who had figured out a thing he was curious about. On the next call he asked follow-up questions about the logistics. Two weeks later we closed a deal 20% larger than the original scope. Did living in Mexico close it? No idea. It certainly did not hurt.

This chapter is about that shift, and about the harder mechanical problem sitting underneath it: how you actually get from an office to a terrace in Mexico without torching your income or getting fired. If you already work from anywhere and just need the strategy, skim the next couple of sections and drop down to the conversion framework. If you are still tied to a desk, or you have a job you like and a boss who has never seen you work from another country, start here. This is the part where you build the case.

## Your location is evidence, so stop hiding it

Think about your last genuinely productive workday. How much of it happened at your physical desk, and how much happened in your head, on video calls, and through digital tools? When you cracked the hard problem, was it because of the building you were sitting in, or because you had the right information, the right tools, and enough quiet to think?

The pandemic settled an argument a lot of us had been having quietly for years. Location turned out to be close to irrelevant for knowledge work. A Stanford study found remote workers were 13% more productive than their in-office counterparts. Most companies still run on industrial-age assumptions about where work is supposed to happen, and that gap between the reality and the policy is the whole opportunity. While your company clings to geography, you can demonstrate, one delivered project at a time, that your value comes from your output and not your zip code. By the time you ask for remote status, you are pointing at a record rather than asking for a favor.

There is a second thing at work here, subtler than productivity. When you mention that you live in Mexico and are raising bilingual kids, small talk turns into trust. Clients and colleagues read you as resourceful, as someone who solved a puzzle most people only daydream about. You become the person with stories beyond the quarterly numbers, who took a calculated risk and made it work, who is clearly not chasing the next 10% raise out of high-cost-of-living anxiety. Your location signals what you have already accomplished: a life built on competence and intention. It is visible proof that you are doing the thing most people file under "someday."

So the advice from the first section, stated plainly: stop hiding it.

## What staying put actually costs

Remember the math from Chapter 1. A $75,000 salary in Austin buys the purchasing power of $125,000 or more in Mexico. If you are stuck in an office, though, you are leaving behind more than the currency spread.

Start with time. The average American spends 54 minutes a day commuting. That works out to 220 hours a year, close to six full work weeks, spent getting to a place so you can do work you could have done from your kitchen. In Mexico your commute might be the walk to your terrace, a café down the block, or a coworking space five minutes away.

Then there is the compounding cost of waiting. Every year you delay costs you the full arbitrage savings, potentially $148,500 a year in combined tax and lifestyle savings, money that could instead be sitting in index funds. At a 10.33% annual return, the price of waiting climbs fast:

| How long you wait | What the delay costs you |
| --- | --- |
| 1 year | $148,500 (the first year's missed savings) |
| 5 years | $220,040 |
| 10 years | $359,724 |

Each year compounds the loss. Put another way, roughly $12,375 a month in potential savings evaporates while you think it over.

Career math points the same direction. Remote workers report higher job satisfaction (83% versus 64%) and are 40% less likely to quit. Employers have noticed, and the best opportunities increasingly flow to people who have already mastered working from anywhere.

You are not on the fringe if you do this. Roughly a third of the U.S. workforce now works remotely at least part-time. Remote workers earn more on average, are more likely to be in committed relationships, and nearly half of them are raising kids while they do it. At the high end, fields like software, consulting, marketing, and writing are already majority-remote. The reality has moved. Most company policy has not, and that lag is your opening.

## Sarah's six months

You do not have to quit and start over. My friend Sarah is proof of the slower, safer route.

Sarah was a marketing manager at a B2B software company in Seattle. She liked her job. What she did not like was the $2,800 a month she handed over for a studio apartment. Rather than quit, she spent six months quietly converting her role to remote, one defensible step at a time.

She started by working from home one day a week, framed as time to "focus on deep work projects." She made sure those were her most productive days on record, documented every result, stayed relentlessly responsive to the people still in the office, and made a point of showing her manager what came out of each one. A couple of months in, she proposed a two-day-a-week remote "trial," pitched as a way to improve her work-life balance and shorten her commute. She tracked her productivity metrics religiously and sent weekly reports showing her output holding or climbing.

By the fifth month she had four months of hard data behind her, and she proposed going fully remote. The pitch was all business: higher productivity, lower overhead for the company, her commitment to stay on Pacific time for meetings, and her offer to fly in quarterly to keep face time with the team. They agreed to a three-month trial. She over-delivered on every single commitment, kept up the video check-ins, and made herself the most responsive person on the team.

When the trial ended, her manager had nothing to argue with. Sarah had become more valuable remote than she had been in the building. She negotiated permanent remote status and moved to Mexico City, where her $85K salary gives her the lifestyle of someone earning $140K or more in Seattle.

## Your conversion framework

The steps Sarah followed are the same ones you will use. Here is the stripped-down version.

**Step 1: Audit your role (Week 1).** List every task you do in a typical week. Mark the ones that genuinely require physical presence, which will be fewer than you expect. Write down the tools you use. Identify the performance metrics your manager actually cares about.

**Step 2: Build your case (Weeks 2 to 4).** Read your company's remote work policy. Find people inside the company who already work remotely and note how they did it. Set up a simple way to measure your own productivity. Draft a short pilot-program proposal.

**Step 3: Start small (Month 1).** Ask for one work-from-home day a week. Make it your most productive day of the week, every week. Share the results before anyone asks. Gather feedback.

**Step 4: Expand gradually (Months 2 to 3).** Move to two or three remote days a week. Keep measuring and keep reporting. Over-communicate. When a concern comes up, handle it immediately rather than letting it fester.

**Step 5: Make your pitch (Month 4).** Present the data. Propose full remote work with a trial period attached. Address the objections before they are raised. Keep the emphasis on what the company gets.

**Step 6: Run the trial (Months 5 to 6).** Over-deliver. Keep the regular check-ins. Document everything that goes right. Be the most responsive person on the team.

If you are building a business instead of converting a job, the shape is different but the discipline is the same.

**Step 1: Validate remotely first.** Start the business as a side project. Choose a model that works across borders. Test it without geographic constraints, and build systems that do not depend on your body being in a particular place.

**Step 2: Calculate your freedom number.** Figure out the minimum monthly income you need to live abroad. Apply the arbitrage multiplier. Set a transition timeline, and build a buffer of six to twelve months of expenses before you leap.

**Step 3: Choose your base.** Research internet reliability. Consider time-zone overlap with your clients. Weigh visas and cost of living. Plan a three-to-six-month test period before you commit.

**Step 4: Execute the transition.** Give proper notice to clients and colleagues where it applies. Move operations over gradually. Test every system before you rely on it. Have backup plans for the infrastructure that matters.

## My own path, for the entrepreneurs

Not everyone should build a business, and not everyone wants to. But if you feel the pull, there is a second road, and it happens to be the one I walked.

In early 2008 I was a Sales Director at a Fortune 500 healthcare company in San Francisco. Good salary, decent benefits, and a commute that quietly ate years I am not getting back. Every day felt like Groundhog Day: comfortable, predictable, and slowly draining. I started building a website during the SEO heyday, not because I hated the job but because I wanted options. Tim Ferriss's *The 4-Hour Work Week* had just come out and it rewired how I thought about work. The idea that you could design a business for mobility, automate the systems, and optimize for a life rather than for maximum revenue read to me like a blueprint.

After several months of nights and weekends I had a proof of concept but needed room to scale it. The breakthrough was arithmetic. My small side income would have enormous purchasing power in Argentina compared to what it bought in San Francisco. My wife and I could live comfortably on a fraction of our Bay Area spending. So I gave my employer two months' notice and we moved to Patagonia on our savings. Rent was $500 a month for a one-bedroom in a picturesque mountain town called San Martín de los Andes. Coffee was $1. Dinner at a nice restaurant ran $8. By month 12 I had stopped dipping into savings and was building the thing that eventually became the eight-figure software company I run today with a team of 15.

The arbitrage did more than save money. It bought me the time to build something that would have been impossible under San Francisco expenses.

## Your minimum technical safety net

You do not need a data center to work from Mexico. You need three things: a primary high-speed connection, a credible backup (a mobile hotspot or Starlink), and a VPN that lets you log into U.S. banks and healthcare portals without a fight.

The detailed setup, meaning specific providers, cross-border payment logistics, and backup power, lives in Chapter 17. For now your only job is to confirm two things: that your target city has at least two viable internet options, and that you have tested a VPN against your critical accounts before you arrive, not after.

The software stack is easier, because all of it is cloud-based and location-agnostic. The one rule is to test each tool from Mexico before you depend on it.

- **Communication:** Slack, Teams, or Google Chat for async messages. Zoom, Microsoft Teams, or Google Meet for video calls. Loom for asynchronous updates. WhatsApp for informal chat.
- **Productivity:** Asana, Monday, or Notion for project management. Google Drive or Dropbox for files. 1Password or Bitwarden for passwords.

## The objections, answered

You already know the arguments against this, because you have been rehearsing them. Here they are, with straight answers.

*"My clients will think I'm unprofessional."* The opposite happens. They think you are interesting. Mention on a call that you are planning a move to Mexico and watch the conversation improve. I have closed bigger deals and built stronger relationships since I started being transparent about working from here.

*"My employer won't allow it."* You have not asked yet. Follow Sarah's framework and let the data make your case. Most companies care about output, not location. You just have to prove the output does not suffer.

*"The internet isn't reliable enough."* Reliability comes from your setup, not your country. With redundant connections and decent infrastructure you will have fewer outages in Mexico than you probably had on basic cable at home. Chapter 17 covers the specifics.

*"Mexico is in a different time zone and I need to stay synced with my team."* Most of Mexico runs on Central Standard Time with no daylight saving. Half the year you line up with Central Time in Chicago, and the other half you line up with Mountain Time in Denver. Setting aside the U.S. border regions, the main exceptions are Baja California (Northern Baja), which follows Pacific Time including daylight saving, and Baja California Sur (Southern Baja), which sits one hour behind the rest of Mexico with no daylight saving. Even a one- or two-hour offset from your home team tends to become a quiet advantage: you get undisturbed work blocks while everyone else is still waking up, and async tools cover the rest. Your calendar ends up looking like someone who thought this through, because you did.

*"What if something goes wrong?"* You already answer this every day at work by building systems so that a failure is manageable instead of catastrophic. In Mexico that means a backup SIM, tested VPN credentials, and a little infrastructure redundancy. Boring, practical stuff. It works.

## What happens when you show up

The infrastructure matters. The frameworks matter. The data matters. The real change, though, happens when you stop hiding and start showing up as someone who has already solved the puzzle.

The people who land the highest-trust assignments are rarely the ones talking about moving "someday." They are the ones whose calendars and Zoom backgrounds already signal a life built around location independence. To an employer or a client, that reads as competence and stability. If you can hold a life like that together, you can probably hold a project together. Your value comes from your skills and your output, not your proximity to a building. The infrastructure for location independence already exists, and the economics are overwhelming. The only open question is whether you will use these systems or let them use you.

### Your next action

By the end of this week, do these three things. Each one is designed to manufacture proof: proof for your manager, proof for your clients, and proof for yourself.

1. **Audit your role and build the case.** Use the framework above to list your tasks, flag the ones that truly need presence, and note your key metrics. Then read your company's remote work policy and find the people inside who already work remotely.
2. **Set up and test your stack.** Stand up the basic tools from this chapter, and if entrepreneurship is on the table, calculate your freedom number while you are at it.
3. **Open the conversation.** Schedule a talk with your manager about increasing your flexibility, starting with a single work-from-home day. You are not asking for a favor. You are proposing a business arrangement that benefits everyone.

Geographic arbitrage begins with one decision: to stop hiding where you work and start proving what you can do from anywhere. Once "this is working" is undeniable, Chapter 8 walks you through turning it into "this is home."
