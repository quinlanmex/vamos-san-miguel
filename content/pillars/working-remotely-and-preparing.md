# Working Remotely and Preparing to Move to Mexico

You do not have to quit your job to move to Mexico. The proven route is to convert your existing role to remote over about six months, one defensible step at a time, building a record of productivity your manager cannot argue with, then run a three-to-four-month preparation sequence to get your finances, possessions, and logistics ready before you board the plane. Location turned out to be close to irrelevant for knowledge work, and while most company policy still lags that reality, that lag is your opening.

This page combines the remote-conversion strategy with the practical mechanics of an international move. Work the two together: prove the work travels, then prepare the life to travel with it.

## How do I convert my job to remote work?

The playbook is a gradual, data-driven case. One marketing manager followed it over six months, started with a single work-from-home day, documented every result, and negotiated permanent remote status before moving to Mexico City, where her $85,000 salary bought the lifestyle of someone earning $140,000 or more in Seattle. Here is the stripped-down version.

1. **Audit your role (Week 1).** List every task, mark the few that genuinely require physical presence, note your tools, and identify the metrics your manager actually cares about.
2. **Build your case (Weeks 2 to 4).** Read the company's remote policy, find people inside who already work remotely, and set up a simple way to measure your productivity.
3. **Start small (Month 1).** Ask for one work-from-home day a week and make it your most productive day, every week. Share the results before anyone asks.
4. **Expand gradually (Months 2 to 3).** Move to two or three remote days, keep measuring and reporting, and handle concerns immediately.
5. **Make your pitch (Month 4).** Present the data, propose full remote work with a trial period, and keep the emphasis on what the company gets.
6. **Run the trial (Months 5 to 6).** Over-deliver, keep the check-ins, document what goes right, and be the most responsive person on the team.

If you are building a business instead of converting a job, the discipline is the same: validate the model remotely first, calculate your freedom number (the minimum monthly income you need abroad, then apply the arbitrage multiplier), choose your base for internet reliability and time-zone overlap, and build a six-to-twelve-month buffer before you leap.

## What technical setup do I need to work from Mexico?

You need three things: a primary high-speed connection, a credible backup (a mobile hotspot or Starlink), and a VPN that lets you log into U.S. banks and healthcare portals without a fight. Before you arrive, confirm your target city has at least two viable internet options, and test your VPN against your critical accounts before departure rather than after.

The software stack is easier because it is all cloud-based and location-agnostic. Communication runs on Slack, Teams, Zoom, or Google Meet, with WhatsApp for informal chat. Productivity runs on Asana, Monday, or Notion, with Google Drive or Dropbox for files and 1Password or Bitwarden for passwords. The one rule is to test each tool from Mexico before you depend on it.

On time zones, most of Mexico runs on Central Standard Time with no daylight saving, so half the year you line up with Chicago and the other half with Denver. A one- or two-hour offset from your team tends to become a quiet advantage, giving you undisturbed work blocks while everyone else is waking up.

## What are the three phases of preparing to move?

A move works when you run three overlapping phases in order, each building the base for the next. Most families need three to four months from decision to departure.

- **Phase 1: Financial and Legal Foundation (3 to 4 months out).** Set up banking, taxes, legal domicile, and healthcare, the systems that take time to process changes. Decide first whether changing your legal domicile to a no-tax state makes sense, because it affects every other step and usually needs two to three months of documented residency.
- **Phase 2: Physical Transition (1 to 2 months out).** Reshape your material life. Decide what stays, what goes, and what travels. This phase needs emotional processing time, not just logistics.
- **Phase 3: Final Logistics (2 weeks out).** Handle the time-sensitive tasks: travel bookings, final document checks, VPN testing, device backups, and mail forwarding.

## What should I do about my house, car, and possessions?

Sort everything through one question, "Does this add value to the life I am building?", and assign each item to one of four categories: Pack It or Panic (documents, medications, electronics, work gear, in 2 to 3 suitcases), Profit and Part Ways (anything over $100, sold individually), Pass It On (donated), and Toss the Guilt (discarded or shredded).

For your house, run the arithmetic before emotion gets a vote. Three options fit different plans:

- **Rent it out.** Most U.S. homes rent for more than a full Mexico budget, so a $2,800-a-month Jacksonville rental easily covers a $1,500 Mexico budget while you keep building equity. Self-management from abroad is workable with tools like Zillow Rental Manager, or hire full-service management at 8 to 12% of rental income.
- **Sell for maximum liquidity.** The cleanest break and the most cash for setup costs. Expect a slow market, roughly 60 to 120 days from listing to closing.
- **Keep it empty (strategic storage).** Preserves flexibility, but do the math: an empty house bleeding $2,500 a month costs $30,000 a year, enough to fund two years of Mexico living.

For your car, weigh selling (maximum cash), storing (a stored car needs monthly attention or it depreciates fast), or renting through Turo (only works with reliable local support).

## What should I pack versus buy in Mexico?

Mexico's cost structure inverts American assumptions, so packing is a financial decision.

| Category | Strategy | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electronics | Bring everything | Import duties and thin retail competition make Mexican replacements a budgeting shock. |
| Clothing | Bring seasonal essentials | Good clothing exists but often costs more due to import fees. Winter layers for mountain towns are not optional, since January nights run into the 30s and 40s Fahrenheit. |
| Kitchen items | Buy there | Cheap and available everywhere. |
| Bedding and towels | Buy there | Bulky to ship, readily available. |
| Medications | Bring a 90-day supply | Bridges you while you find local equivalents. |
| Documents | Bring originals | Birth and marriage certificates, diplomas, tax returns (last 3 years). Keep digital copies in cloud storage. |

If you are bringing a pet, the rules center on a current rabies vaccination, parasite treatment within six months, and a SENASICA inspection on arrival, with a USDA-accredited health certificate for dogs. These rules shift often, so confirm the current SENASICA and USDA requirements before you book rather than trusting any single blog post.

## What if preparation reveals I am not ready?

That is a valuable outcome, not a failure. One person in the book started sorting possessions and discovered her aging parents needed her nearby and her business required a physical presence she had not accounted for. She learned crucial information before burning bridges and buying one-way tickets. Moving abroad is an optional financial and lifestyle optimization, not a moral test. Some families execute in twelve weeks, others need eighteen months to build savings or wait for a school year to end, and neither is wrong. Preparation works both directions: some people find they are more ready than they thought, others find they need more time.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I ask my employer to let me work remotely from Mexico?**
Do not ask for a favor, build a record. Follow the six-step framework: start with one work-from-home day, make it your most productive day, document the results, expand gradually, then pitch full remote with a trial attached. By the time you ask, you are pointing at data rather than requesting permission. Most companies care about output, not location.

**Will working from Mexico hurt my professional reputation?**
The opposite tends to happen. Clients and colleagues read a location-independent life as competence and resourcefulness. One author closed a deal 20% larger than the original scope after mentioning he was working from Oaxaca. Stop hiding where you work and let your setup signal that you have already solved a puzzle most people only daydream about.

**How long does it take to prepare for the move?**
Most families need three to four months, run in three phases: financial and legal foundation (3 to 4 months out), physical transition (1 to 2 months out), and final logistics (2 weeks out). Compress it much faster and you miss critical steps; stretch it much longer and you lose momentum.

**Should I sell my house or rent it out?**
Both work. Renting brings in roughly twice what a comparable place costs in Mexico while you keep building equity, and self-management from abroad is workable. Selling gives the cleanest break and the most cash for setup, but expect 60 to 120 days to close. Keeping it empty is the expensive option: an empty house at $2,500 a month costs $30,000 a year.

**What should I pack and what should I buy in Mexico?**
Bring all electronics (import duties make replacements costly), seasonal clothing and winter layers for mountain towns, a 90-day medication supply, and original documents. Buy kitchen items, bedding, and towels there, since they are cheap, available, and bulky to ship.
