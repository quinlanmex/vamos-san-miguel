# Mexican Residency and Visas: The Complete Guide for Americans

Most Americans qualify for Mexican residency through the income-or-savings path, and for a clean case with complete paperwork, approval is routine. You will generally need to show around US$4,000 to $4,400 a month in provable income (or roughly US$70,000 in savings) for temporary residency, and the whole process runs about 8 to 18 weeks from your first document to a residency card in your hand. Over a million Americans already live in Mexico, the largest U.S. expat population in the world, and Mexico built these pathways on purpose to attract financially independent residents.

This page maps how the system works and how residency connects to your tax position. Every threshold and fee here is a moving target pegged to Mexican formulas that drift upward each year and get interpreted differently from one consulate to the next, so confirm the current numbers with the specific consulate that will process you before you assemble a single document.

## Am I eligible for Mexican residency?

Four routes lead to residency, and most people take the first.

- **Income or savings (the common path).** For temporary residency, expect your consulate to want roughly US$4,000 to $4,400 a month in provable deposits, or around US$70,000 or more in savings held consistently over 12 months. The income has to come from outside Mexico. For permanent residency, many consulates look for roughly US$7,000 to $7,300 a month, or savings in the high US$200,000s.
- **Property.** Own debt-free Mexican property above a high threshold, typically a high six-figure USD value backed by official valuations and deeds in your name. This is the high-asset route, not the "I bought a modest house" route.
- **Family connections.** A Mexican spouse, children, or parents, with lower financial requirements and sometimes none.
- **Business investment.** A substantial business investment in Mexico, with more complex documentation.

Thresholds swing hard by consulate and exchange rate. The figures above are 2025-era ballparks, nothing more.

## What is the difference between temporary and permanent residency?

Mexico runs a clean two-tier system, and the mechanics are nearly identical across both.

- **Temporary Resident Visa (1 to 4 years, renewable).** Lets you live in Mexico full-time and keep receiving income from a foreign employer or business, as long as you are not taking Mexican-based employment. It is also the on-ramp to permanent residency.
- **Permanent Resident Visa (indefinite).** Never expires. It becomes your platform for naturalization after several years, commonly five (shorter if you are married to a Mexican citizen).

Most people start temporary and upgrade later. It is often the easier entry and buys you time to decide whether you want to stay for good.

## Does the whole family have to qualify financially?

No, and this is the provision that changes the math for families: only one family member needs to meet the financial requirements. The qualifying spouse applies as the main applicant, the other applies as a dependent, and both receive identical residency status. All minor children (under 18) come along as dependents with no added financial threshold. So a family of four, at many consulates, needs one person showing roughly US$4,000 to $4,400 a month, not four times that. Some consulates add a modest per-dependent increment, which is one more reason to confirm with yours directly. Bring apostilled marriage and birth certificates for every family member, and explicitly request dependent status at your appointment.

## How long does the residency process take?

The whole thing runs in three phases, and the total timeline is 8 to 18 weeks.

1. **Document preparation (4 to 6 weeks).** Apostilled birth certificate, apostilled marriage certificate if applicable, a criminal background check where required, passport photos, and for the income path, six months of bank statements plus a CPA letter explaining your income. The background check is often the slowest part, so start it first, and order only the exact report your consulate wants.
2. **Consulate application (2 to 8 weeks).** Confirm your chosen consulate will process your case (they differ widely, and you may travel to a neighboring country or a border city for a friendlier one), submit your packet, pay fees typically $50 to $200, and sit for a brief interview.
3. **Entry and finalization (2 to 4 weeks).** Once the visa sticker is in your passport, you have 180 days to enter Mexico, then 30 days to visit an INM office inside Mexico, submit final paperwork, and receive your physical residency card.

## How much does the temporary resident visa cost?

Consulate fees typically run $50 to $200 depending on visa type, plus government fees at the INM stage. If you hire help, professional facilitators run $1,500 to $2,500 for an individual application and $2,500 to $4,000 for a family, usually covering everything except government fees and certified translations. Translation adds another $500 to $1,000. One family of four in the book spent roughly $4,000 total: about $2,500 for the facilitator, $500 to $1,000 for translations, and around $500 in government fees.

Compare that to the cost of avoiding residency. One American completed nine border runs in four years to keep renewing a tourist visa, each costing $500 or more, which is $4,000 to $5,000 burned just to stay technically legal, without ever realizing residency was available to him.

## How do I present my income at the appointment?

The fix is language, not fiction. Mexican immigration law assumes people are employees, business owners, retirees, or investors, and it has no box for "digital nomad" or "remote worker." Terms like "freelancer" and "remote worker" sound to an officer like active employment that could compete with a Mexican citizen for a job. Present the same truthful facts in the framework the law contemplates.

| Instead of this | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| "I'm a digital nomad" | "I operate a location-independent business" |
| "I freelance" | "I own a consulting business" |
| "I work remotely" | "I receive income from my U.S.-based business" |
| "I'm between jobs" | "I'm financially independent from my investments/savings" |

Be truthful, be specific about where your money comes from, present it in business terms, and make clear you will not compete for a Mexican job. Line up your documents to match: business registration, client contracts, bank statements showing consistent deposits, and a CPA letter.

## Why does residency unlock everything else?

Residency is the foundation the rest of your financial life is built on. With it you get a residency card, a CURP, and, when you need it, an RFC tax ID, exactly the documents that banks, notaries, and utilities want before they treat you as a long-term participant. It also gives you the stable home base that supports a long-term Foreign Earned Income Exclusion strategy.

Here is the piece that trips people up: your immigration status and your tax residency are separate questions. Holding a residency card and living most of the year in Mexico does not automatically make you a Mexican tax resident. Whether you owe Mexican income tax depends on your residency status under Mexican law, your entity structure, and where your work is physically performed. When your economic life is genuinely centered in the United States (a real U.S. dwelling, 100% U.S.-source income, consistent documentation across both countries), Mexican domestic law and the U.S.-Mexico tax treaty tend to resolve toward U.S. residency even if you spend most of the year physically in Mexico. This is position selection under uncertainty, and you should reach it deliberately with a cross-border CPA rather than inherit it from the person next door.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much income do I need for Mexican temporary residency?**
Expect roughly US$4,000 to $4,400 a month in provable deposits from outside Mexico, or around US$70,000 or more in savings held over 12 months. Permanent residency generally wants about US$7,000 to $7,300 a month or savings in the high US$200,000s. Confirm current figures with your specific consulate, because they drift upward every year.

**Can my spouse and kids get residency if only I qualify?**
Yes. Only one family member needs to meet the financial requirements. The other spouse applies as a dependent, minor children come along with no added threshold, and everyone receives identical status. Bring apostilled marriage and birth certificates and request dependent status at the appointment.

**How long does it take to get a Mexican residency card?**
About 8 to 18 weeks from your first document to the card: 4 to 6 weeks for document prep, 2 to 8 weeks for the consulate application, and 2 to 4 weeks for entry and finalization at an INM office inside Mexico.

**Do I owe Mexican income tax if I have a residency card?**
Not automatically. Immigration status and tax residency are separate. Whether you owe depends on your residency under Mexican law, your entity structure, and where your work is performed. A U.S.-centered economic life often resolves toward U.S. tax residency under the treaty. Decide it with a cross-border CPA.

**Should I hire a facilitator or do it myself?**
If you are single, bilingual, bureaucracy-tolerant, and your finances are simple, do it yourself. If you have a family, complex income, or limited time, a facilitator ($1,500 to $2,500 individual, $2,500 to $4,000 family) sharply reduces the odds of rejection or delay over a technicality.
