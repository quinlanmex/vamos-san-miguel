# The Honest Safety Picture in San Miguel de Allende

A visitor from Denver joined us on a boat through the canals of Xochimilco, waved off my suggestion to skip the street quesadilla, and spent the next three months back home paying for it. That one bite is the whole safety conversation in miniature. Americans arrive worried about the dramatic threat that lives on geography they will never set foot on, and walk right past the boring daily one that lands in their body. The point here is to spend your worry where it changes an outcome.

## Is San Miguel de Allende safe?

Start with the error underneath every other error, which is treating Mexico as one place with one safety level. Mexico is the thirteenth-largest country in the world, organized into 32 states, and it has as many security profiles as the United States does. The U.S. State Department writes its advisory state by state for exactly this reason. As of the August 2025 update, Mexico as a whole sits at Level 2, "Exercise Increased Caution." Six states carry the top "Do Not Travel" rating because they are trafficking corridors, and at the other end Campeche and Yucatán sit at Level 1, the same rating given to most of the world.

San Miguel de Allende, in Guanajuato, runs on a different logic entirely, the kind where a neighborhood WhatsApp chat decides who waters whose plants while a family travels. You already accept this reasoning at home, where Baltimore and a quiet resort town in Arizona sit in the same country and live completely different lives. Calling Mexico dangerous because one state is works exactly as well as calling America dangerous because one city is. Your safety math is set by which town, which neighborhood, which street.

## What are the real daily risks?

The threats the media trains you to fear operate where you will not be. The ones that will actually shape your first six months are almost entirely inside your control, and mostly invisible to American instinct. In the book's own framing, violent crime in trafficking corridors is very high in perceived severity and near zero in actual daily impact for someone in the wrong geography. Water contamination and food-borne illness rank low in perceived severity and high in real impact. Night highway driving lands in the middle on both. The pattern is hard to unsee once you read it: worry gets spent almost exactly backwards from where it belongs.

A small illustration of the upside. The authors' teenagers move around San Miguel with more independence than they had in an American suburb, walking to friends' houses and homeschool meetups. The low background hum about random violence that the family carried in the States is simply gone.

## Why is water the thing to plan for?

The foundation of your safety system is not a wall or an alarm. It is your digestive health, which touches daily life more than any other risk on the list. The water is not sourced from anywhere strange. The issue is treatment and storage: water often arrives treated, then gets pumped into rooftop tanks that bake in the sun, where heat, standing water, and plastic build a bacterial load. Understanding the mechanism turns water management from paranoia into a habit you keep without resenting it.

The daily practices are plain. Rent a place with filtration if your town has it, either whole-house or an under-sink reverse-osmosis unit. Default to bottled water, keep your mouth closed in the shower, and brush your teeth with filtered water. Wash all produce with a disinfectant like Microdyn, soaking and then rinsing with filtered water. The riskiest food is rinsed, not cooked, so early on lean on cooked food from established, popular vendors and treat rinsed vegetables from unknown kitchens as the gamble. A note on the probiotic routines the book describes: those are one family's personal protocol, cleared with their own doctor, not medical advice, so run anything like it past your own doctor first.

## How does neighborhood safety actually work here?

Mexico's expat hubs run on community-based security instead of urban anonymity. Everyone knows everyone, most homes and shops have cameras, and shopkeepers remember your face. One morning a police officer knocked on the authors' door in San Miguel, apologized for the interruption, and asked to see their Ring footage after a motorcycle theft; between their video and a few neighbors' cameras, the thief was picked up by that afternoon. That is the texture of crime in an expat-friendly neighborhood, mostly non-violent petty theft, worked with real urgency and modern tools.

Your real safety system is the web of people who know you. In week one, learn your neighbors' names and numbers, join the WhatsApp colonia chat (this, not sirens or apps, is how neighborhood safety runs day to day), and save two trusted taxi numbers as backups. The high-risk behaviors travel with you anywhere: buying drugs on the street, flashing wealth in unfamiliar places, wandering alone at night in a neighborhood you do not know. The low-risk daily stuff is exactly what it sounds like: walking the town center in daylight, taking registered taxis or rideshare, shopping in established markets, showing up to community events.

## What about driving and emergencies?

Do not drive the highways at night. Poor visibility, unmarked speed bumps, and unpredictable road conditions add up to a real hazard that daytime alertness will not undo. Daytime driving is fine. Night driving is a risk you can simply delete from your life, and the irony is that this is the thing most Americans never even search before they move, while it threatens the body more than every cartel headline they did read.

Learn the hospital landscape before you need it. As an expat you will usually default to mid-tier or top-tier private care, and many international insurance plans are built to cover exactly those hospitals, so call and confirm the network before you move. Some private hospitals ask for a credit-card deposit before treatment, which is normal practice here, so ask about the policy in advance rather than discovering it while you are in pain. In a true life-threatening emergency, call 911 first, since Mexico uses 911 too.

## The real takeaway

The enemy is accumulation, not catastrophe. One contaminated salsa will not wreck your transition, but fifteen of them will, and most expats who struggle blame Mexico rather than the daily protocols they never put in place. Get the systems dialed in and safety stops occupying your mind at all. Roughly speaking, your Mexico safety system is about 80 percent digestive health, about 15 percent ordinary social awareness, and about 5 percent everything the internet wants you to lose sleep over. Get those proportions right and many expats discover they have moved somewhere that feels calmer in practice than the suburb they left.
