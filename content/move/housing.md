# Housing in San Miguel de Allende: Renting and Buying

Our first condo in Mexico had a view that stopped conversations and a twenty-foot drop off one side of the balcony that a toddler treated as an invitation. We noticed it on day two, after we had signed. We fixed it with planters and hired help, and we learned the lesson that runs through this whole subject: the house you pick is the multiplier on everything else you build. Get it right and daily life runs quietly in the background. Get it wrong and you spend your energy managing the house instead of living in it. The good news is that the mistakes are learnable in advance.

## How do rentals actually work in San Miguel de Allende?

The best long-term rentals do not appear on the sites you already know. They live in the heads and phones of local realtors who hold quiet relationships with owners and often have access to the most desirable properties. If you search only the familiar sites, you are seeing the leftovers. Contact at least two realtors, since they tend to represent different portfolios, and weight how connected they are above how much you personally click with them. A well-connected agent who has worked the same town for years can surface listings that never hit the market.

When you reach out, be specific about property type, monthly budget (include utilities if you can, since Mexican utility costs swing hard with usage and season), furnishing level, kitchen reality, neighborhood, space, and climate control. Mexican "furnished" ranges from complete down to the silverware to a bed and a table, so define your terms. Then go see every serious contender in person, in daylight and after dark, on a weekday and a weekend, because photos lie with a straight face about space, light, and noise. If a landlord will allow it, a one-week trial before signing surfaces what a viewing never will.

## What is the Mexico-specific wrinkle in a rental?

Ask who pays for gardening and exterior maintenance, which is almost always the landlord, and whether an existing housekeeper comes with the house. In some rentals the landlord already has a trusted housekeeper on fixed days whom you pay directly, often in the range of $3 to $5 an hour. Those answers give you a truer picture of your real monthly cost and your weekly time savings, so ask three plain questions: who pays for gardening and exterior maintenance, is there an existing housekeeper and who pays them, and can you opt out of regular housekeeping if you would rather.

## What should I know before signing the lease?

Mexican rental agreements run on different assumptions than American ones. Get the lease in both English and Spanish, and understand that the Spanish version is the one that binds you, since a contract needs to be in Spanish to be enforceable here. Run both versions line by line to confirm they say the same thing, and fix any divergence before you sign.

Standard practice is one month's deposit plus first month's rent upfront. Negotiate the deposit-return timeline while you are at it, since landlords often propose 60 days and fifteen days after move-out is more reasonable. Put the payment method in the contract; transfers through a service like Wise create documentation and are generally reliable, while cash leaves no paper trail. In some markets, especially at higher price points, owners ask for a fiador, a local co-signer who owns debt-free property in the same state. Most new expats do not have one, and you can often negotiate around it with a larger deposit, several months paid upfront, or a letter of reference. Do not be shy about the rent itself, since Mexican landlords are often far more flexible on price than their American counterparts, and the worst answer is no.

## Why does the exchange rate change my rent?

Most long-term leases quote and pay rent in pesos, which is usually what you want since your daily spending is in pesos too. The hidden variable is the exchange rate. A 35,000-peso cabin cost the authors about $1,400 a month when the dollar bought 25 pesos, and $2,060 two years later when the peso strengthened to 17, a difference of nearly $700 a month. The walls did not move; the effective rent did. You can stay in pesos and accept the swings, negotiate a defined band that adjusts annually, or in rare cases quote the rent in dollars directly. The point is to be intentional rather than stumble into a lease that feels affordable at today's rate and turns painful when the peso moves.

## Should I choose a gated fraccionamiento or a traditional neighborhood?

In an expat-heavy town like San Miguel, you will run into fraccionamientos, gated communities with controlled access, security staff, maintained landscaping, and often a pool or gym. The appeal is real and immediate: you move in and you are not alone, with people already there to ask about doctors and restaurants. The trade-off is that most sit on the outskirts, so the walkable, historic, market-rich parts of town are a car ride away, and the friction points that turn a visitor into a resident quietly vanish.

A fraccionamiento serves you well when you are establishing stability with young children, your Spanish is still limited, you are staying a year or less, or you simply value operational certainty over immersion. It costs you when you want community beyond the expat bubble, you value walkability, or you are planning to stay long-term, when the gates can start to feel like a fence. The honest pattern is that many families start in one for the psychological comfort of arrival, then move into a traditional neighborhood once their confidence and Spanish catch up. Others skip them entirely and take the friction from day one. Both are legitimate. Choose on purpose.

## What about buying, and the infrastructure reality?

The authors rented fully furnished for three years before buying their own home, which kept them nimble while they found the right spot. Whether furnished or unfurnished, the only real mistake is defaulting into one by accident instead of choosing it on purpose. Whichever way you go, treat the infrastructure gap as part of the adjustment rather than a defect. Learn your specific water setup and confirm the rooftop tanks have been cleaned. Outlets and voltage match the United States, so your devices plug in and work, but power outages are more common than you are used to, so plan for battery backup from the beginning. Test actual internet speeds during peak hours rather than trusting the brochure number.

One more surprise: addresses often barely exist outside the major cities, reading more like directions than a street number, so expect to lean on WhatsApp location sharing and map pins. Your relationship with your landlord runs well past the signing, and in Mexican culture personal relationships shape business outcomes, so pay on time, report issues clearly, and treat the property with obvious care.
