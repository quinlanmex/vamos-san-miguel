# Healthcare and Insurance in San Miguel de Allende

Our two-year-old took a fall onto concrete in our first month in Mexico. Hours later, after a haircut finally revealed the swelling the curls had hidden, we walked into a private hospital that looked more like a hotel. A pediatric team saw us right away, ran a CT on the same equipment we had seen at top U.S. hospitals, found a hairline fracture that needed watching rather than surgery, and the doctor wrote her cell number on the back of her card. Total time from haircut to diagnosis was two hours. That afternoon taught me what care looks like when it is built around patients, and it also taught me not to confuse a good experience with a finished plan.

## Is healthcare good in San Miguel de Allende?

Routine care in Mexico is where the system is at its best, and San Miguel offers reasonable options backed by the larger city of Querétaro nearby for anything specialized. You pay cash for the clarity of knowing the price before you walk in. The book's working figures: a doctor consultation runs $15 to $25, prescription antibiotics $3 to $8, a basic blood panel $25 to $40, an X-ray $15 to $30, and a dental cleaning $25 to $50. That is often less than a U.S. copay before anyone touches a deductible.

Quality does cluster around cities and tourist zones and thins out in remote areas. San Miguel sits in a good spot for an expat hub, with a large community and the Querétaro backstop close by. The honest planning input is to match the town to the care you actually need, rather than assume every place delivers the same thing.

## How much does insurance cost, and do I even need it?

The mistake that costs the most is going bare. One expat cancelled all U.S. coverage to save $400 a month, then a single emergency, a procedure her area did not offer plus an out-of-country hospital stay and surgery, ran to about $23,000. Nearly five years of premiums gone in one event. The lesson she drew is the right one: a healthcare strategy has to work in both countries.

The book frames coverage as four separate problems. Pillar one is local routine care, which you handle by paying cash. Pillar two is cross-border emergency coverage, the layer that keeps a ruptured appendix or a car accident from bankrupting you. Pillar three is specialty and tertiary care access, for the cardiology consult or cancer treatment a small town cannot provide. Pillar four is catastrophic protection, the do-not-lose-the-house layer. Knowing which pillar you are trying to fill is what tells you which product makes sense, because some options solve one and others solve several.

## Which insurance pathway is right for me?

The question that sorts most people is simple. Do you need ongoing U.S. specialist relationships or specific U.S. medications? If yes, a U.S. PPO with international benefits often wins. If no, comprehensive international coverage usually beats a U.S.-centric plan and can cost less while covering all four pillars.

Three pathways come up. A U.S. PPO with out-of-network international benefits (major insurers offer these) suits families with pre-existing conditions who need continuity with U.S. specialists. You pay upfront in Mexico and submit receipts for partial reimbursement. International plans with U.S. networks (companies like GeoBlue and Cigna Global) provide comprehensive worldwide coverage that for healthy families often costs less than carrying separate policies. A hybrid keeps a minimal U.S. catastrophic policy alongside an international plan. Pre-existing conditions shift the whole equation, so when the authors ran their own numbers, keeping a U.S. PPO with international benefits came out cheaper once ongoing medication needs were factored in.

Generic advice fails here because your health history, medications, travel pattern, and family size make a one-off equation. Get real quotes from more than one pathway on your actual situation, be completely honest about every condition and medication on the application, and call your current insurer more than once, because many agents are poorly trained on international benefits and will tell you the wrong thing.

## What do the bigger procedures cost in Mexico?

For the larger events, Mexico offers well-equipped facilities at a steep discount, with many hospitals accredited to international standards. Reporting on medical travel puts the overall savings at roughly 36 to 70 percent below U.S. prices. The manuscript's own working ranges sit inside that band: an appendectomy around $3,000 to $5,000 in Mexico against $15,000 to $25,000 in the U.S., and a normal delivery around $1,500 to $3,000 against $8,000 to $15,000. Treat these as orders of magnitude, not quotes, and confirm current prices with the specific hospital.

## What if I live far from a major hospital?

Medical evacuation insurance earns its keep if you live more than two hours from a major hospital, or if you plan high-adventure activities in remote areas. Premiums typically run a few hundred dollars a year, often under $400. For families in a major expat center it is usually an unnecessary line item. Match the product to your actual distance from care.

## Turning coverage into a system you can use

Coverage on paper is not the same as coverage you can reach at 2 a.m. In your first week, map your emergency resources: the nearest hospital with a 24/7 emergency department, urgent-care clinics, and 24-hour pharmacies. Confirm that 911 reaches emergency services where you are, since it is the single national emergency number across Mexico. Build a contact list with your insurer's international emergency line, a trusted local doctor, and consular medical services. Prepare your documents: insurance details you can find fast, a medical history translated into Spanish, and a current medication list using generic names, since brand names differ between countries.

Then run one low-stakes test in your first month. Book a dental cleaning or a basic blood panel and walk your whole system through once while nothing is wrong. That single dry run is what carries you through the day something actually breaks. When the strategy is clear and tested, you stop thinking about healthcare and start living.
