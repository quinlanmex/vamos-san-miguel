# Healthcare and Insurance in San Miguel de Allende

Our two-year-old took a fall onto concrete in our first month in Mexico. Hours later, after a haircut finally revealed the swelling the curls had hidden, we walked into a private hospital that looked more like a hotel. A pediatric team saw us right away, ran a CT on the same kind of equipment we had seen at top U.S. hospitals, found a hairline fracture that needed watching rather than surgery, and the doctor wrote her cell number on the back of her card. Total time from haircut to diagnosis was two hours. That afternoon taught me what care looks like when it is built around patients, and it also taught me not to confuse a good experience with a finished plan.

## Is healthcare good in San Miguel de Allende?

Routine care in San Miguel is where the system is at its best, and it is backed by the larger city of Querétaro nearby for anything specialized. The town has real capacity of its own. On the public side there is the state-run Hospital General Dr. Felipe G. Dobarganes, plus IMSS and ISSSTE clinics for people affiliated through work, and IMSS-Bienestar coverage for residents who are not. On the private side, San Miguel has full-service hospitals including Hospital MAC, which opened in 2017 with emergency, intensive care, and specialists across roughly two dozen fields, and Hospital Joya, which opened in 2021 with emergency and intensive care and its own roster of specialties. Many of the private doctors speak English. Alongside the hospitals sit numerous private clinics and consulting doctors for everyday visits.

For anything major, complex, or highly specialized, the practical answer is Querétaro, about an hour away by car, where several large hospitals hold international accreditations and carry a deeper bench of specialists. Expats here routinely handle daily care in San Miguel and drive to Querétaro for the cardiology workup, the cancer treatment, or the surgery a smaller town does not staff for. Plan for that geography rather than assume every service sits inside the city limits.

Most expats pay cash for routine private care, and the appeal is knowing the price before you walk in. Verified San Miguel figures: a consultation with a private, English-speaking doctor generally runs about 500 to 1,000 pesos, roughly $25 to $55 at current exchange rates, while the doctor attached to a pharmacy chain costs only 45 to 60 pesos, about $2 to $3, for minor issues. Prescription medications typically run 30 to 70 percent below U.S. prices, and pharmacies are everywhere, with many common drugs available without a prescription. Telemedicine services with licensed Mexican doctors are widely available and useful for prescriptions and minor questions. A routine private visit here is often less than a U.S. copay before anyone touches a deductible.

### Care options in and near San Miguel

| Option | What it covers | Rough cost / access |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public IMSS / ISSSTE | Routine and hospital care for people affiliated through employment | Funded through work; Spanish-first; longer waits |
| IMSS-Bienestar | Public care for legal residents not covered by IMSS/ISSSTE who enroll | Low or no direct cost once enrolled; Spanish-first |

| Querétaro hospitals (~1 hour away) | Complex and tertiary care, wider specialists, internationally accredited | Cash or insurance; common referral point for major care |
| Pharmacies + telemedicine | Over-the-counter meds, minor consults, online doctors | Meds ~30–70% below U.S.; telemedicine widely available |

## How much does insurance cost, and do I even need it?

The mistake that costs the most is going bare. One expat cancelled all U.S. coverage to save on premiums, then a single emergency, a procedure her area did not offer plus an out-of-country hospital stay and surgery, ran into the tens of thousands of dollars and erased years of savings. The lesson she drew is the right one: a healthcare strategy has to work in both countries.

Think of coverage as four separate problems. Pillar one is local routine care, which you handle by paying cash in San Miguel. Pillar two is cross-border emergency coverage, the layer that keeps a ruptured appendix or a car accident from bankrupting you. Pillar three is specialty and tertiary care access, for the cardiology consult or cancer treatment you would drive to Querétaro or a U.S. center for. Pillar four is catastrophic protection, the do-not-lose-the-house layer. Knowing which pillar you are trying to fill is what tells you which product makes sense, because some options solve one and others solve several.

## Which insurance pathway is right for me?

The question that sorts most people is simple. Do you need ongoing U.S. specialist relationships or specific U.S. medications? If yes, a U.S. PPO with international benefits often wins. If no, comprehensive international coverage usually beats a U.S.-centric plan and can cost less while covering all four pillars.

Three pathways come up. A U.S. PPO with out-of-network international benefits suits families with pre-existing conditions who need continuity with U.S. specialists. You pay upfront in Mexico and submit receipts for partial reimbursement. International plans with U.S. networks, from companies like GeoBlue and Cigna Global, provide comprehensive worldwide coverage that for healthy families often costs less than carrying separate policies. A hybrid keeps a minimal U.S. catastrophic policy alongside an international plan. Pre-existing conditions shift the whole equation, so run your own numbers rather than trust a rule of thumb.

Generic advice fails here because your health history, medications, travel pattern, and family size make a one-off equation. Get real quotes from more than one pathway on your actual situation, be completely honest about every condition and medication on the application, and call your current insurer more than once, because many agents are poorly trained on international benefits and will tell you the wrong thing.

## What do the bigger procedures cost in Mexico?

For the larger events, Mexico offers well-equipped private facilities at a steep discount, and many hospitals are accredited to international standards. Reporting on medical travel consistently puts the savings well below U.S. prices, often by half or more, though the exact figure depends entirely on the procedure and the hospital. Treat any number you read as an order of magnitude, not a quote. Before you count on a price, call the specific hospital in San Miguel or Querétaro and ask what your procedure actually costs there today.

## What if I live far from a major hospital?

Medical evacuation insurance earns its keep if you live more than two hours from a major hospital, or if you plan high-adventure activities in remote areas. Premiums typically run a few hundred dollars a year. For someone based in San Miguel, with private hospitals in town and Querétaro's larger hospitals about an hour away, it is usually an unnecessary line item. Match the product to your actual distance from care.

## Turning coverage into a system you can use

Coverage on paper is not the same as coverage you can reach at 2 a.m. In your first week, map your emergency resources: the nearest hospital with a 24/7 emergency department, urgent-care clinics, and 24-hour pharmacies. Confirm that 911 reaches emergency services where you are, since it is the single national emergency number across Mexico. Build a contact list with your insurer's international emergency line, a trusted local doctor, and consular medical services. Prepare your documents: insurance details you can find fast, a medical history translated into Spanish, and a current medication list using generic names, since brand names differ between countries. If you expect to use private hospital care, call ahead and confirm which San Miguel and Querétaro hospitals are in your plan's network, and ask each whether it requires a credit-card deposit before treatment, which is normal practice here.

Then run one low-stakes test in your first month. Book a dental cleaning or a basic blood panel and walk your whole system through once while nothing is wrong. That single dry run is what carries you through the day something actually breaks. When the strategy is clear and tested, you stop thinking about healthcare and start living.
