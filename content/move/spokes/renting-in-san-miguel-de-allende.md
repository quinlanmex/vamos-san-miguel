# Renting in San Miguel de Allende: How the Market Works, Where to Look, Lease Norms

Most people rent before they buy in San Miguel de Allende, and renting well starts with knowing that the best places are not where you would think to look. The strong long-term listings sit off the international sites entirely. This page covers how the market works, where to find the good rentals, what deposits and leases look like, and the Mexico-specific questions that change your real monthly cost.

## How does the rental market work in San Miguel de Allende?

The best long-term rentals do not appear on the sites you already know. They live in three places: with local realtors who hold quiet relationships with owners, in the San Miguel rental Facebook groups where "se renta" posts move within hours, and on the walls of the neighborhood you want, where owners still hang paper signs. If you search only the familiar international sites, you are seeing the leftovers.

A few habits make the search work:

- **Contact at least two realtors.** They tend to represent different portfolios, so two agents surface more of the market than one.
- **Weight connectedness over rapport.** How wired into the owner network an agent is matters more than how much you personally click with them.
- **Watch the Facebook groups daily.** Good listings there move within hours, so slow responders lose them.
- **Walk the neighborhood.** The classic move still works. Signs on walls point to rentals that never touch the internet.

## Where should you look, and what should you ask for?

When you reach out, be specific about property type, monthly budget, furnishing level, kitchen reality, neighborhood, and space. Mexican "furnished" ranges from complete down to the silverware to a bed and a table, so define your terms rather than assume. Then go see every serious contender in person, in daylight and after dark, on a weekday and a weekend, because photos lie with a straight face about space, light, and noise. A Centro apartment charming at noon can sit above a loud bar at night. If a landlord will allow it, a one-week trial before signing surfaces what a viewing never will.

## What deposits and upfront costs should you expect?

Standard practice is one month's deposit plus first month's rent upfront. A few points are negotiable, and it pays to raise them before signing:

- **Deposit-return timeline.** Landlords often propose 60 days. Fifteen days after move-out is more reasonable, so negotiate it into the contract.
- **Payment method.** Put it in the contract. Transfers through a service like Wise create documentation, while cash leaves no paper trail.
- **The fiador.** At higher price points, owners sometimes ask for a fiador, a local co-signer who owns debt-free property in the same state. Most new expats do not have one, and you can often negotiate around it with a larger deposit, several months paid upfront, or a letter of reference.
- **The rent itself.** Do not be shy about negotiating price. Mexican landlords are often more flexible than their American counterparts, and the worst answer is no.

## What should you know before signing a Spanish lease?

Mexican rental agreements run on different assumptions than American ones. Get the lease in both English and Spanish, and understand that the Spanish version is the one that binds you, since a contract needs to be in Spanish to be enforceable here. Run both versions line by line to confirm they say the same thing, and fix any divergence before you sign.

Two Mexico-specific questions change your real monthly cost, so ask them plainly:

- **Who pays for gardening and exterior maintenance?** It is almost always the landlord, but confirm it.
- **Is there an existing housekeeper, and who pays them?** In some rentals the landlord already has a trusted housekeeper on fixed days whom you pay directly, often around 60 to 90 pesos an hour. Ask whether you can opt out if you would rather.

## How does the exchange rate affect your rent?

Most long-term leases quote and pay rent in pesos, which is usually what you want since your daily spending is in pesos too. The hidden variable is the exchange rate. A lease that feels affordable at today's rate turns more expensive in dollars if the peso strengthens, and the walls never move. You can stay in pesos and accept the swings, negotiate a defined band that adjusts annually, or in rare cases quote the rent in dollars directly. Be intentional rather than stumble into a number that feels comfortable now and painful later.

## Frequently asked questions

**Where are the best rentals listed?** With local realtors, in the San Miguel rental Facebook groups, and on signs in the neighborhood. The good long-term places largely skip the international sites.

**How much deposit is normal?** One month's deposit plus first month's rent upfront is standard. Negotiate the return timeline down from a proposed 60 days toward 15 days after move-out.

**Does the lease have to be in Spanish?** Yes. A contract needs to be in Spanish to be enforceable in Mexico. Get an English version too, but know the Spanish one binds you.

**Should I rent furnished or unfurnished?** Furnished keeps you nimble while you learn the town, but "furnished" varies widely in Mexico, so confirm exactly what comes with the place before you sign.
