# Life in San Miguel de Allende

Our current base is San Miguel de Allende, and it is far from perfect. It runs pricey by Mexican standards, it fills up during peak season, and a few neighborhoods can feel like an expat bubble. For our family the ledger still balances. The education options for teens are excellent, the cultural calendar rivals major U.S. cities, and the climate of warm days and cool nights is close to ideal for our life stage. That gap between how the same town reads to different families is what alignment means. Here is what daily life here actually involves.

## The day moves on foot

The morning it clicked for me, I was winding through the cobblestones just after sunrise, past shopkeepers sweeping doorsteps and the smell of fresh tortillas. By the time I sat down for breakfast I had covered more ground than I used to on a full day back in the States, without once calling it a workout. San Miguel is built on hills and built before cars decided how streets should work, which turns a grocery run into something close to interval training whether you signed up for it or not.

Walking is not a fitness plan on its own, and staying strong still means lifting something heavy on purpose. That is cheaper here too. Neighborhood gyms run 300 to 800 pesos a month, roughly $15 to $40, for access that would cost $150 or more in a U.S. city, and a kettlebell in the corner with a 20-minute video works just as well on a bad day. The point is that movement stops being a decision you win every morning and becomes the shape of the day.

## Eating well without the fight

Mexico's first Michelin guide arrived in 2024 and has been expanding across the country since, validating what anyone paying attention already knew about the depth of the food here. The dining runs a full spectrum, from street vendors and family mom-and-pop kitchens to chef-driven rooms for a date night. Learn to read a stall (high turnover, visible preparation, a genuinely busy vendor) and food safety becomes a skill you build rather than a gamble you take.

Two local customs save awkwardness. Tipping runs on different math than in the States, where 15 percent is the appreciated sweet spot and 20 percent reads as the edge of excessive. And the check never arrives on its own, because bringing it early reads as pushing you out, so ask for it with "La cuenta, por favor" when you are ready.

## Raising kids here

For families, San Miguel offers something American suburbs have mostly lost: a childhood organized around participation in real life rather than performance inside scheduled slots. Every afternoon around six our neighborhood changes gears. Families come out for walks, elderly couples claim their benches, and children move freely through the adult landscape, weaving between conversations and finding a game in every corner. Our kids absorbed a web of relationships that would have taken years to build in an American suburb, and they built a durable kind of confidence that comes from handling real things: ordering in Spanish, navigating a market, recovering from a mistake in public.

None of this is automatic, and Mexico is not a developmental cheat code. Specialized services like speech therapy are patchier and often mean telehealth or a trip to a larger city. A U.S.-style college-admissions profile will not build itself, so plan for intentional test prep. And expect a language-frustration window of three to six months. The most common parental mistake is dialing back Spanish immersion right when the frustration peaks. The breakthrough waits on the far side of it. Families land in different places, from keeping American structure with Mexico as the backdrop to full immersion, and the right choice depends on your kids rather than anyone's ideal.

## Plugging into the community

You are never really alone in an expat-friendly town, and San Miguel has one of the deepest expat infrastructures in the country. The community is already built, laid down by the Americans who arrived before you, and you can start using it before you ever book a flight through Facebook groups, WhatsApp communities, and meetups. It catches you when you fall. It can also harden into a comfortable cage that keeps Mexico at arm's length for years.

The expats who do this well tend to move through three phases: survival in the first six months, when the community is your oxygen tank; integration from six to eighteen months, as Mexican friendships form and the community becomes a choice; and contribution beyond that, when you are the one answering the panicked messages. A few people never leave survival mode and build an insular bubble that, oddly, makes Mexico feel more foreign rather than less. The goal is to treat the infrastructure as scaffolding and take it down once the building stands. Watch out for the helper with an agenda, the drama magnet, and the bubble reinforcer who dismisses Mexican customs as inefficient. Get a second opinion on every major decision, and pursue Mexican friendships on purpose.

## A first home, not a forever verdict

Hold the choice loosely. Your first Mexican base is not your last, and plenty of expats cycle through two or three towns as they learn the difference between what they wanted and what they thought they wanted. That progression is the journey teaching you, and it takes the paralysis out of choosing perfectly. If San Miguel fits the life you are living now, it is a remarkable place to start.
