# Is Moving to San Miguel de Allende Worth It? An Honest Gut-Check

We ran the numbers one night at our kitchen table in California. We earned well, lived without luxury cars or a boat, and still could not see a path to retiring early. It sounded like it should be absurd. It sounded like math. Federal and state income tax alone took a large share of what we made before we paid a mortgage or bought groceries. My wife asked the question that reframed everything: what if we are talking about the wrong problem? This page is the honest gut-check that came out of fifteen years of testing the answer. It is written to help you decide whether this move is for you, not to talk you into it.

## Who is this move actually for?

Geographic arbitrage works for some households and not for others. After fifteen years of testing locations inside and outside the U.S., living abroad was the clear winner for our family, freeing up a substantial share of our income as savings each year, not by earning more but by our willingness to step outside our comfort zone. The math is real. Math does not move you, though. Decisions move you, and the decision rests on four questions that have nothing to do with tax exclusions or compounding curves.

Can your income come with you? The whole strategy collapses if your income is tied to a location you cannot leave. Fully portable income (freelance, contract, business, or an employer who permits international remote work) means you are ready. A surgeon, courtroom litigator, or anyone whose work requires physical presence at a specific place may not fit this move right now.

Where in Mexico would you actually live? Mexico is not one place. The financial math is roughly the same across the country, but the lifestyle math varies enormously. A family with school-age kids needs something completely different from a single 30-year-old or a 60-year-old pre-retiree. The wrong destination is the most common reason expat moves fail in the first year.

Who else has to say yes? If you are partnered, the strategy requires both people genuinely on board, not just willing to go along. Moves fail in month four when the less-enthusiastic partner finally admits they never wanted to be there. If you have kids, their ages matter. If you have aging parents, know before you go how you would handle a 3 a.m. call that says you need to be there tomorrow.

What is your runway? The move saves money over time, and it costs money in the first six to twelve months. Budget six months of full living expenses as a minimum cushion, twelve is better. With less than three months and no income lined up, financial pressure colors every early decision.

## Why San Miguel de Allende specifically?

Mexico sits in the sweet spot among global options. Costs run dramatically below the U.S. while the support infrastructure has no equal: somewhere between 1.5 and 2 million U.S. expats already live here, the largest American population abroad anywhere, and the temporary-to-permanent residency path has been worn smooth by thousands of Americans a year. Proximity keeps visits home cheap. Within Mexico, San Miguel sits in the temperate highlands, the Goldilocks zone where highs stay in the low-to-mid 80s and mornings and nights turn crisp, so the weather almost never becomes the obstacle. It is in the moderate-arbitrage tier, where you are likely to save 40 to 50 percent against a comparable U.S. lifestyle, often with a quality-of-life upgrade on top.

## What does San Miguel de Allende ask in return?

The authors are honest that their own base is far from perfect. San Miguel runs pricey by Mexican standards, it fills up during peak season, and a few neighborhoods can feel like an expat bubble. For their family the ledger still balances: excellent education options for teens, a cultural calendar that rivals major U.S. cities, and a near-ideal climate for their life stage. For another family, that same San Miguel would read as expensive and shallow. That gap is not a verdict on the town. It is what alignment means. Choose for the life you are living now, not for an idealized future self, and hold the choice loosely, since your first Mexican base is not your last.

## What is the honest downside?

Executing this takes real effort. The setup phase (visa research, tax planning, location selection, housing) runs roughly 100 hours of focused work, and successful expats then budget 10 to 15 hours a year for compliance and bureaucratic navigation. The hidden failure point is not visas or paperwork. It is the psychological weight of relocation hitting around month two or three, when novelty fades and every small inconvenience feels like evidence you made a mistake. People who underestimate that cost abandon the move before the math ever has time to compound. The expats who succeed treat the first year as a committed experiment, not a trial run they can abort on bad days. And the real place only shows up in its worst month, which is why a four-week trial in the ugliest season, living your ordinary life rather than a vacation, is the single most useful test you can run before committing.

## How do I know if I am ready?

Answer the four questions honestly. Four green lights and the full relocation guide becomes your operating manual. Two or three greens with one or two unknowns, and the unknowns are exactly what to dig into before you go further. Two or fewer greens is worth knowing too, because the math does not expire. Some people start this process three years before they actually move, using the time to renegotiate their work, sell a house, or wait for a kid to graduate.

There is a wider point that outlasts San Miguel itself. Once you have figured out visas, housing, and daily life in one foreign country, you own a portable skill you can point at almost anywhere, whether that stays San Miguel or someday becomes somewhere else entirely. Most people who ask the "where else could I do this" question stay, and the value is in what asking it does to your head.

The move is not for everyone, and it does not pretend to be. If your income can come with you, your household is genuinely choosing it together, you have named the version of you making the decision, and you have a runway, then the honest answer for many families is yes, it is worth it. If you cannot answer those yet, you now know exactly which parts to work through first.
